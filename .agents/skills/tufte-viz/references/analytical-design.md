# Analytical design

Working interpretations of ideas in Tufte's *Envisioning Information*, *Visual Explanations*, and *Beautiful Evidence*. Use these for dense or explanatory displays when relevant; do not turn them into mandatory features of a simple metric plot.

## Comparison and explanation

Identify the comparison the reader should make. For a composite metric, a contour plot can show tradeoffs between components. To explain differing metrics, hold the relevant conditions constant rather than changing several causes at once. Show a mechanism only when the data or construction supports it; visual association alone is not causal evidence.

Additional variables help when they explain the question. A simple one-variable demonstration can be appropriate and should not gain extra encodings for their own sake.

## Layering

Make the main data prominent and keep grids, references, and secondary series subordinate but readable. Use weight, color, and spacing to separate roles. The need for clearer hierarchy does not automatically justify deleting a useful reference or changing the book's palette.

## Context and detail

A display can reveal an overall pattern and individual exceptions. Small multiples, selective annotation, and tables can support this without crowding every point with a label. Document what was measured, units, sources, and simulation assumptions where needed.

Sparklines can summarize trends alongside text or table values. Use full axes when readers need exact values or unfamiliar units. A range frame can communicate the observed extent of a line or scatter series; it should not remove a meaningful baseline or imply an incorrect range.

Judge the final rendering by whether the intended comparison is clear. Integration of words and graphics should help readers understand the evidence, not hide essential explanations in tiny labels.
