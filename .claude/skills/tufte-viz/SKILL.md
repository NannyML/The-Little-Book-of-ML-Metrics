# Tufte-informed visual judgment

Begin with what the reader needs to compare or understand. Inspect the existing display before recommending changes. For this book, **book-plot** owns NannyML styling and printed-size checks; this skill does not authorize plot edits during writing-only work.

Use the principles to improve comprehension, not to maximize a score:

- Show values honestly through appropriate scales, baselines, units, and context. Distinguish observed association from an established mechanism.
- Make relevant comparisons easy. Small multiples help when consistent views reveal differences; a simple chart can be sufficient for a simple idea.
- Reduce distracting decoration while keeping labels, guides, and whitespace that help readers. More data per square inch is useful only while the display remains readable.
- Use color purposefully and consistently with the book's palette. Grayscale is an option, not a requirement.
- Integrate annotations with data where practical. Direct labels, legends, and explanatory captions each have legitimate uses.
- Preserve meaningful 3D surfaces; distinguish them from decorative depth that distorts a two-dimensional comparison.

For a critique, identify a specific reading problem, propose a change, and explain its benefit and tradeoff. Compare the rendered before and after. Do not replace a plot because it looks "too simple," has a legend, or lacks multiple variables.

Read [graphical principles](references/tufte-principles.md) for scales and data density, or [analytical design](references/analytical-design.md) for small multiples, layering, and dense displays. These are working interpretations, not quotations or universal rules for every figure.
