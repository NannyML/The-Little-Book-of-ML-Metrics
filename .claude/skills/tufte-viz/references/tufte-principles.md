# Graphical principles in practice

These working notes interpret ideas from Edward Tufte's *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*. They guide choices; they are not a claim that every figure needs the same form.

## Integrity

Show the quantities actually measured. Label units, groups, aggregation, and uncertainty where relevant. Keep scales comparable across panels intended for direct comparison. Bar lengths generally need a zero baseline; line and scatter plots may use a narrower clearly labeled range when that serves the task without exaggeration.

Check area and volume encodings carefully: scaling a radius with a value also squares the represented area. Decorative depth can distort a comparison; a surface representing two inputs and a metric value has a different purpose.

The lie factor compares a graphic's represented relative effect with the underlying relative effect. It can expose disproportionate encodings where that calculation is meaningful, but it is not a universal numerical test for every chart. Check the actual encoding and context.

## Useful ink

Lighten heavy grids, remove redundant borders, and avoid decoration that competes with the data. Keep context the reader needs: reference lines, units, category labels, and explanations can justify their space even when they do not encode additional observations.

Ask whether removing an element makes the comparison equally easy to understand. Do not erase it simply because the data would still technically be present.

## Density and small multiples

Small multiples help compare conditions with consistent scales and visual encoding. Leave enough separation to distinguish panels and read labels. Shared axes can save space when the relationship remains unambiguous.

Show more data when it adds relevant evidence; do not fill a small educational figure merely to make it dense. Inspect the display at its final printed size. Whitespace and a few carefully chosen cases can teach more clearly than a crowded sweep.
