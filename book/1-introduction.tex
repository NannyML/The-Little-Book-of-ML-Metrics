\chapteropener{Introduction}{cyanlight}{cyanaccent}{%
A metric is a decision about what counts as good. Choose mean squared error and a forecast that is badly wrong once a month will look
worse than one that is slightly wrong every day; choose accuracy on a rare disease and a model that never diagnoses anyone will score
$99\%$. This book exists because those decisions are usually made by default, and the default is rarely examined. It covers some
hundred metrics across ten domains, one or two pages each, and every page tries to answer the same three questions: what the number
measures, when it is the right number to watch, and where it will mislead you.}{}

\section{Who this book is for}

The reader we had in mind builds or monitors machine learning systems and already knows what a confusion matrix or a residual is.
The pages are not tutorials; they are the notes a senior colleague might give you before a model review, with the trade-off spelled
out and a worked number where a number helps. If a metric is unfamiliar, its page is meant to make it usable in an afternoon; if it
is familiar, the strengths, weaknesses and ``did you know'' box are where the less obvious facts live.

The book is also a reference. Metrics are grouped by the kind of model they score and each chapter opens with a short guide to the
family and a list of its pages, so it can be read from a chapter's first page or opened at the metric you were sent to look up.

\section{How to read a metric}

Every metric follows the same two-page layout, so that the same question is always in the same place.

\textbf{Page one} states what the metric tells you, in plain language and with a concrete example; gives the formula with each
symbol annotated; explains how to interpret the value, with the range and the anchors that make a number good or bad; says when to
use the metric and, just as important, when to avoid it and what to use instead; and ends with two boxes, strengths on the left,
weaknesses on the right, each with a concrete case rather than an adjective.

\textbf{Page two} shows the metric at work. The figure is never a decoration: it is built to expose the mechanism, how the metric
turns inputs into a score and what kind of error it rewards or forgives, and every number printed on it is computed from the data it
draws. The caption teaches how to read the figure. A ``did you know'' box holds one fact that changes how you think about the
metric, and ``other related metrics'' explains what it means when this metric and a neighbouring one disagree.

The Data Observability chapter is the exception: its monitors are simple statistics whose value lies in their history, and each
takes one page.

\section{The colors in the formulas}

Symbols in every formula are colored by role, and the arrows name them:

\begin{itemize}[left=0pt]
    \item {\color{nmlcyan}\textbf{Cyan}} marks what is actually true: targets, labels, ground-truth values, correct predictions.
    \item {\color{nmlpurple}\textbf{Purple}} marks what the model produced: predictions, scores, estimated values, errors.
    \item {\color{nmlred}\textbf{Red}} marks sizes and counts: the number of samples, classes, bins or items that normalize the rest.
    \item {\color{nmlgreen}\textbf{Green}} marks special functions such as the indicator $\mathds{1}[\cdot]$.
    \item {\color{nmlyellow}\textbf{Yellow}} marks parameters you choose: thresholds, weights, exponents, quantile levels.
\end{itemize}

The same convention holds across chapters, so a purple $\hat{Y}$ in a regression formula and a purple $\hat{y}$ in a fairness
criterion are the same kind of object.

\section{Choosing a metric}

Three questions settle most choices, and each page's ``when to use'' section is organized around them.

\textbf{What is the cost of each kind of error?} A regression metric decides whether a few large misses outweigh many small ones
(MSE against MAE); a classification metric decides whether a false alarm or a miss is the expensive mistake (precision against recall,
or the expected cost directly). If you cannot name the cost, the metric is choosing it for you.

\textbf{What is the base rate?} Rare positives make accuracy and ROC AUC optimistic and make precision-recall curves, MCC and balanced
accuracy informative; small targets make percentage errors explode; different base rates between groups make most fairness criteria
mutually exclusive. Look at the class balance before the metric.

\textbf{What will the number be compared with?} A metric in the target's own units (MAE, RMSE) is for stakeholders; a scale-free one
($R^2$, MAPE, nDCG) is for comparing models across datasets; a proper scoring rule (log loss, Brier) is for training and calibration.
The same model can deserve all three, reported to different people.

When two metrics that should agree do not, the disagreement is information. Each page's closing section names the most common
disagreements and what they reveal: RMSE far above MAE means a few large errors; ROC AUC high with PR AUC low means the minority
class is being missed; CBPE steady while realized accuracy falls means the input--label relationship changed.

\section{How the book is organized}

Chapters follow the kind of model being scored. \textbf{Regression} and \textbf{Classification} cover the metrics almost every
practitioner meets; \textbf{Clustering} and \textbf{Ranking} cover unsupervised partitions and ordered lists; \textbf{Computer Vision}
and \textbf{NLP} cover pixels, masks, keypoints and token overlap; \textbf{GenAI} covers generated text, images and audio, where
references are scarce and learned judges take over. \textbf{Probabilistic} turns the question around and estimates a metric when
labels are missing; \textbf{Bias \& Fairness} asks whether the score is the same for every group; \textbf{Data Observability} steps
back to the tables that feed everything else.

Every figure in the book is generated by code in the accompanying repository, from the data it shows, with the NannyML palette and
as little ink as the point allows. Where a figure's numbers matter they are repeated in the caption, so the page can be read without
squinting.
