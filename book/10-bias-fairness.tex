\chapteropener{Bias \& Fairness}{coralhard}{coralaccent}{%
Bias and fairness metrics help you check whether a model's decisions and errors differ across groups, such as age groups or genders. Overall accuracy can hide these differences: a model may work well for most people while repeatedly making mistakes for a smaller group. The five criteria in this chapter compare selection rates, error rates, or the meaning of a prediction. Meeting one criterion does not establish that a system is fair in every respect.

Two ideas help organize these comparisons. \emph{Separation} means that, among people with the same actual outcome, prediction rates are the same across groups. \emph{Sufficiency} means that, among people given the same prediction, actual outcome rates are the same across groups. These criteria can conflict when groups have different base rates, meaning different proportions of positive outcomes.

The figures use a simulated screening model and two groups with different proportions of qualified people. Each criterion checks a different part of its predictions, so the model can pass one check and fail another. Use the criteria to make those differences visible and decide which matter for the people affected.}{In this chapter}

\section{Demographic Parity}\label{sec:demographic-parity}
\subsection{Demographic Parity}

Demographic parity requires the same proportion of positive predictions in every group, regardless of the actual outcomes. In a hiring screen, for example, it compares the share of applicants selected from each group. Equal selection rates do not tell you whether the model selected the right people.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b) \quad \forall\, a, b
                $
            }
        };
        \path[room for labels] ([yshift=0.28cm]a.north) ([yshift=-0.48cm]a.south);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.30,-0.03)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {predicted outcome} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-2.34,0.18)$) to node[pos=1, below] {protected attribute} +(0.00,-0.39);
    }
\end{center}

The formula compares the positive prediction rate for groups $a$ and $b$, identified by the attribute $A$. This is the independence criterion: knowing someone's group does not change their probability of receiving a positive prediction. In practice, compare group rates using a difference or a ratio.

\textbf{When to use Demographic Parity?}

Use it when equal selection rates are the goal, such as access to an outreach program. It can also help when historical labels are unreliable. If the priority is recognizing qualified people equally often, use Equality of Opportunity; if it is the correctness of positive predictions, use Predictive Parity.

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Needs only predictions and group labels, so you can compare selection rates before actual outcomes become available.
\item A rate difference shows the gap directly: selection rates of $30\%$ and $20\%$ differ by ten percentage points.
}
{
\item Ignores correctness. Two groups can have equal selection rates even if many more selected people are unqualified in one group.
\item Equal selection rates may conflict with predicting the labels accurately when positive outcomes occur at different rates across groups.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Demographic_Parity.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Demographic Parity.} Each square represents one person in a simulated screening task; each group has $100$ people. The black outline contains everyone selected by the model: blue squares are qualified people and red squares are unqualified people. With the original threshold, it selects $54$ people from Group A and $32$ from Group B, so demographic parity fails.

The right-hand panel lowers Group B's threshold until it also selects $54$ people. The selection rates now match, but $25$ of B's selected people are unqualified, compared with four in A. Parity checks the size of the selected share, not whether those selections are correct.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Random selection can satisfy demographic parity without using any information about who is qualified. Giving everyone a $40\%$ chance of selection produces equal selection rates in expectation, though observed rates will vary in finite samples. This is why demographic parity alone cannot establish that a model makes useful decisions.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Demographic Parity compares selection rates across whole groups. Equality of Opportunity instead compares selection among qualified people, while Predictive Parity compares how many selected people are qualified. Looking at these together helps distinguish equal access to selection from equal treatment of qualified people and equally accurate selections.

\thispagestyle{biasfairnesstyle}
\section{Equality of Opportunity}\label{sec:equality-of-opportunity}
\subsection{Equality of Opportunity}

Equality of opportunity requires the same true positive rate (TPR) across groups. It focuses on people whose actual label is positive. For example, a loan model meets this criterion if it approves the same proportion of applicants who would repay in each group, even when the overall approval rates differ.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \path[room for labels] ([yshift=0.48cm]a.north) ([yshift=-0.35cm]a.south);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.01,-0.02)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.16)$) to node[pos=1, above] {actual outcome} +(0.00,0.30);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{center}

The formula compares positive predictions only among people with $Y=1$. It is one part of separation: Equality of Opportunity checks the positive class, while Equality of Odds checks both classes. Matching TPR therefore leaves false-positive rates unconstrained.

\textbf{When to use Equality of Opportunity?}

Use it when missed positives are the main concern, such as overlooking qualified applicants for interviews or admissions. Check Equality of Odds as well when false positives cause harm. Both criteria rely on actual labels, so first consider whether those labels measure the outcome you intend.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Compares how often qualified people are recognized without requiring equal selection rates across the whole population.
\item Can be improved by adjusting group-specific thresholds without retraining, although exact matching may require randomized decisions.
}
{
\item Does not constrain false positives: a model can match TPR while incorrectly selecting unqualified people more often in one group.
\item Equal rates can still be poor rates. A TPR of $20\%$ in every group satisfies the criterion while missing most positive cases.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{figures/Equality_of_Opportunity.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Equality of Opportunity.} Each square represents one person in a simulated screening task. The outlined area contains the qualified people: $60$ in Group A and $30$ in Group B. Filled blue squares are selected; empty blue squares are missed. The model selects $50$ of $60$ in A and $25$ of $30$ in B, giving both a TPR of about $83\%$.

Equality of opportunity holds because these proportions match, even though the counts differ. The faded squares represent unqualified people, whose predictions do not enter this calculation.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Equality of opportunity does not require a model to recognize every qualified person. It only requires the recognition rates to match across groups. If both groups have a TPR of $83\%$, about one in six qualified people is still missed in each. Report the rates themselves alongside their difference so that equal performance is not mistaken for good performance.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Equality of Odds adds a check on false-positive rates. If Equality of Opportunity holds but Equality of Odds fails, the remaining difference is in how often negative cases are incorrectly selected. Demographic Parity compares overall selection rates, so it can disagree with either criterion when the groups contain different proportions of qualified people.

\section{Equality of Odds}\label{sec:equality-of-odds}
\subsection{Equality of Odds}

Equality of odds, also called equalized odds, requires both the true positive rate and the false positive rate to match across groups. It checks whether the model recognizes positive cases equally often and incorrectly flags negative cases equally often. Unlike Equality of Opportunity, it accounts for both types of error.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \node[below=0.1cm of a, font=\normalsize] {$\forall\, y \in \{0, 1\},\; \forall\, a, b$};
        \path[room for labels] ([yshift=0.48cm]a.north) ([yshift=-0.35cm]a.south);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.02,-0.02)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.16)$) to node[pos=1, above] {actual outcome} +(0.00,0.30);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{center}

The formula applies separately to $Y=1$ and $Y=0$, which gives the separation criterion. On an ROC plot, a point records a model's FPR and TPR at a chosen threshold. Groups with equalized odds have the same pair of rates, although they may need different thresholds to reach it.

\textbf{When to use Equality of Odds?}

Use it when both missed positives and false alarms matter, as in fraud detection. If only missed positives are the concern, Equality of Opportunity is a narrower check. If the priority is equally reliable positive predictions, examine Predictive Parity too; matching error rates does not guarantee matching precision.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Checks both positive and negative cases, so equal recall cannot hide a difference in false-positive rates.
\item Uses familiar confusion-matrix rates, making it possible to identify which type of error differs between groups.
}
{
\item Matching both rates can require group-specific thresholds or randomized decisions, and may reduce accuracy.
\item When base rates differ and TPR and FPR are both nonzero, matching those rates gives different precision across groups.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{figures/Equality_of_Odds.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Equality of Odds.} Each square represents one person in a simulated screening task, with qualified people in the upper outlined block and unqualified people below. Among qualified people, the model selects $50$ of $60$ in Group A and $25$ of $30$ in Group B: about $83\%$ in each. Among unqualified people, it selects four of $40$ in A and seven of $70$ in B: $10\%$ in each.

Both TPR and FPR match, so equality of odds holds. The different numbers of qualified and unqualified people mean that equal rates need not produce equal numbers of mistakes.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Matching error rates does not necessarily mean using the same threshold. Groups can have different score distributions, so a shared cutoff may give different TPRs and FPRs. Group-specific thresholds can sometimes match both rates; other cases require randomizing between decision rules. Even identical ROC curves do not guarantee that the same numeric threshold gives the same rates.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Equality of Odds includes Equality of Opportunity because it requires equal TPR as well as equal FPR. Predictive Parity checks precision instead, so compare it separately. Demographic Parity checks overall selection rates, which can differ even when both error rates match.

\section{Predictive Parity}\label{sec:predictive-parity}
\subsection{Predictive Parity}

Predictive parity requires positive predictions to be correct equally often across groups. It compares each group's precision, also called positive predictive value (PPV). If a loan model has a PPV of $80\%$ in each group, then $80\%$ of the applicants it approves in each group would repay.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \path[room for labels] ([yshift=0.55cm]a.north) ([yshift=-0.35cm]a.south);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.01,-0.16)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {actual outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.03)$) to node[pos=1, above] {predicted outcome} +(0.00,0.25);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{center}

The formula compares actual outcomes among people predicted positive ($\hat{Y}=1$). If PPV is equal, the false discovery rate (FDR), or share of positive predictions that are wrong, is equal too: $FDR=1-PPV$. This checks sufficiency for positive predictions only; full sufficiency for binary decisions also checks negative predictions.

\textbf{When to use Predictive Parity?}

Use it when the correctness of positive predictions matters across groups, such as approvals or alerts that lead to further action. Check Equality of Opportunity to see who the model misses. If both error rates matter, also check Equality of Odds rather than assuming equal precision covers them.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Gives a direct comparison of positive predictions: a PPV of $0.8$ means $80\%$ are correct in each group.
\item Can be calculated from each group’s true-positive and false-positive counts without access to prediction scores.
}
{
\item Conflicts with Equality of Odds when base rates differ and both TPR and FPR are nonzero.
\item Equal precision can hide unequal recall: the model may overlook a much larger share of positive cases in one group.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{figures/Predictive_Parity.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Predictive Parity.} Each square represents one person in a simulated screening task. The black outline contains the people the model selects; blue squares are qualified and red squares are not. In Group A, $50$ of the $54$ selected people are qualified, giving a PPV of about $93\%$. In Group B, $25$ of $32$ are qualified, giving about $78\%$. Predictive parity fails, with a gap of about $14.5$ percentage points.

The model has the same TPR and FPR in both groups. Group B contains fewer qualified people and more unqualified people, so its selected set has a larger share of false positives.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Precision depends on how common positive outcomes are, as well as on the model's error rates. With the same nonzero TPR and FPR, a group with fewer positive cases will have lower precision. This explains why equalized odds and predictive parity can conflict. The qualification matters: with no false positives, precision is $100\%$ in every group that receives positive predictions, even if some positive cases are missed.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Predictive Parity checks who is correct among those selected; Equality of Opportunity checks who is selected among actual positives. Equality of Odds adds the false-positive rate. Calibration within Groups checks the meaning of individual probability scores, but calibrated scores do not guarantee equal precision after a threshold is applied.

\section{Calibration within Groups}\label{sec:calibration-within-groups}
\subsection{Calibration within Groups}

Calibration within groups means that predicted probabilities match observed outcome rates in each group. If a model gives people a $30\%$ probability of an outcome, about $30\%$ of those people should experience it in each group. It is not enough for the groups to match each other: the observed rate must also match the predicted probability.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}S} = s,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = s \quad \forall\, s,\, a
                $
            }
        };
        \path[room for labels] ([yshift=0.55cm]a.north) ([yshift=-0.45cm]a.south);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-3.23,0.17)$) to node[pos=1, below] {actual outcome} +(0.00,-0.30);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-1.46,-0.04)$) to node[pos=1, above] {predicted score} +(0.00,0.28);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.14,-0.04)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {protected attribute} +(1.00,0.30);
    }
\end{center}

Here, $S=s$ means the model assigned probability $s$, and $Y=1$ means the outcome occurred. For $s=0.3$, the formula requires an outcome rate of $30\%$ in every group. In practice, we usually group nearby scores into bins and compare their average predicted probability with the observed rate.

\textbf{When to use Calibration within Groups?}

Use it when people interpret scores as probabilities, for example when estimating demand for a follow-up service. Check calibration separately for each group, since an overall result can hide differences. If scores are turned into yes/no decisions, also examine the resulting selection and error rates.

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Makes probabilities comparable across groups: a score of $0.3$ should correspond to a $30\%$ outcome rate in each.
\item Checks scores across their range, rather than assessing decisions at just one chosen threshold.
}
{
\item Does not guarantee equal selection rates, precision, or error rates after a threshold is applied.
\item Requires enough outcomes within each group and score range. Small samples or broad bins can hide calibration errors.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/Calibration_within_Groups.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Calibration within Groups.} The plot groups simulated prediction scores into ranges, shown along the bottom. Each range has a blue column for Group A and a purple column for Group B. The filled squares show the observed proportion of qualified people, rounded to tenths; the numbers above give percentages. The black line marks the score range's midpoint as an approximate calibration reference.

For scores between $0.5$ and $0.6$, about $77\%$ of Group A is qualified, compared with $48\%$ of Group B. The model therefore understates the probability for A and overstates it for B in this range. The scores are not calibrated within these groups.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{A model can be calibrated overall and still be miscalibrated within individual groups. If it underestimates outcomes in one group and overestimates them in another, the differences can cancel when the groups are pooled. Check each group separately, and include sample counts so that a noisy estimate from a small group is not mistaken for a reliable result.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Calibration within Groups implies score-level sufficiency: among people with the same score, outcome rates do not depend on group. Predictive Parity instead pools everyone above a decision threshold, where groups may have different mixes of scores. Use Equality of Odds to assess the resulting error rates, and group-level calibration plots alongside the Brier Score to assess probability predictions.
