\chapteropener{Bias \& Fairness}{coralhard}{coralaccent}{%
Bias and fairness metrics compare a model's performance across groups. They can reveal whether it rejects qualified applicants more often in one group, approves unqualified applicants more often in another, or gives less reliable probabilities. Overall accuracy can hide these differences.

The figures follow a simulated model selecting applicants for an opportunity. Group A has $60$ qualified people out of $100$; Group B has $30$. These proportions are their \emph{base rates}. The model recognizes qualified applicants equally often in both groups, yet a selected applicant is more likely to be qualified in A. The chapter explains why.

We first decide what should be equal. \emph{Demographic parity} requires selecting the same proportion from each group, regardless of qualifications. \emph{Separation} compares qualified and unqualified applicants separately, requiring equal selection rates within each category. \emph{Sufficiency} starts with the decisions: selected applicants should be qualified equally often across groups, and the same should hold among rejected applicants. These approaches check different aspects of decisions, so a model can meet one criterion and fail another.}{In this chapter}

\metricentry{Demographic Parity}{Demographic Parity}\label{sec:demographic-parity}

Demographic parity requires the same proportion of positive predictions in every group. In hiring, for example, if a model advances $30\%$ of male applicants to an interview, it should also advance $30\%$ of female applicants. This comparison does not consider who is qualified, so matching the rates alone does not tell us whether the model chose suitable candidates.

% Formula
\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b) \quad \forall\, a, b
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.30,-0.03)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {predicted outcome} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-2.34,0.18)$) to node[pos=1, below] {protected attribute} +(0.00,-0.39);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The formula compares the positive prediction rate for groups $a$ and $b$, identified by the attribute $A$. This is the independence criterion: knowing someone's group does not change their probability of receiving a positive prediction. In practice, compare group rates using a difference or a ratio.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use it when equal selection rates are the goal, such as access to an outreach program. It can also help when historical labels are unreliable. If the priority is recognizing qualified people equally often, use Equality of Opportunity; if it is the correctness of positive predictions, use Predictive Parity.


\coloredboxes{
\item Needs only predictions and group labels, so you can compare selection rates before actual outcomes become available.
\item A rate difference shows the gap directly: selection rates of $30\%$ and $20\%$ differ by ten percentage points.
}
{
\item Ignores correctness. Two groups can have equal selection rates even if many more selected people are unqualified in one group.
\item Equal selection rates may conflict with predicting the labels accurately when positive outcomes occur at different rates across groups.
}

\metriccaption{Figure. Demographic Parity.}{Each square is one person in a simulated screening task; each group has $100$ people. The outline contains everyone the model selects: cyan squares are qualified, red squares are not. One threshold selects $54$ people in Group A and $32$ in Group B, so demographic parity fails. Lowering B's threshold until it selects $54$ (right) satisfies parity, but $25$ of those are unqualified.}

The right-hand panel lowers Group B's threshold until it also selects $54$ people. The selection rates now match, but $25$ of B's selected people are unqualified, compared with four in A. Parity checks the size of the selected share, not whether those selections are correct.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{A model can satisfy demographic parity without learning who is qualified. If it selects everyone with a $40\%$ probability, both groups have a $40\%$ selection rate in expectation, with some variation in finite samples. There is also a less obvious problem: a model could select the strongest applicants from one group and the weakest from another while keeping the selection rates equal. Demographic parity would not distinguish that model from one that selects equally well in both groups.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Demographic Parity compares selection rates across whole groups. Equality of Opportunity instead compares selection among qualified people, while Predictive Parity compares how many selected people are qualified. A model can select equal shares of two groups while selecting their qualified members at different rates.

\metricentry{Equality of Opportunity}{Equality of Opportunity}\label{sec:equality-of-opportunity}

Equality of opportunity requires equal selection rates among qualified people across groups. For loans, compare applicants who would repay: approving $80\%$ of those men should mean approving $80\%$ of those women. This is the true positive rate. Demographic parity instead compares approval across all applicants, including non-repayers; those overall rates can differ even when equality of opportunity holds.

% Formula
\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.01,-0.02)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.16)$) to node[pos=1, above] {actual outcome} +(0.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The formula compares positive predictions only among people with $Y=1$. It is one part of separation: Equality of Opportunity checks the positive class, while Equality of Odds checks both classes. The groups can therefore have matching TPRs but different false-positive rates.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use it when missed positives matter most, such as overlooking qualified applicants for interviews or admissions. Check Equality of Odds when false positives also cause harm. Both need reliable labels. Repayment is usually observed only for approved loans, so those records alone cannot establish equality of opportunity across all applicants.


\coloredboxes{
\item Compares how often qualified people are recognized without requiring equal selection rates across the whole population.
\item Can be improved by adjusting group-specific thresholds without retraining, although exact matching may require randomized decisions.
}
{
\item Does not constrain false positives: a model can match TPR while incorrectly selecting unqualified people more often in one group.
\item Equal rates can still be poor rates. A TPR of $20\%$ in every group satisfies the criterion while missing most positive cases.
}

\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Equality of Opportunity.}{Each square represents one person in a simulated screening task. The outline contains the qualified people: $60$ in Group A and $30$ in Group B; unqualified people are faded. Filled cyan squares are selected; empty cyan squares are missed. The model selects $50$ of $60$ in A and $25$ of $30$ in B, giving both a TPR of about $83\%$.}

Equality of opportunity holds because these proportions match, even though the counts differ. The faded squares represent unqualified people, whose predictions do not enter this calculation.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Hardt, Price, and Srebro introduced this formulation in $2016$, focusing on access for qualified people. A perfect classifier always satisfies it: every qualified person is selected. Demographic parity can reject that classifier. If $60\%$ of one group and $30\%$ of another qualify, selecting all and only qualified people gives different selection rates, despite making no prediction errors.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Equality of Odds also requires equal false-positive rates. If opportunity holds but odds fails, false-positive rates differ. Demographic Parity compares overall selection rates, which can differ when groups have different proportions of qualified people.

\metricentry{Equality of Odds}{Equality of Odds}\label{sec:equality-of-odds}

Equality of odds, also called equalized odds, requires both the true positive rate and the false positive rate to match across groups. In hiring, the model should select the same proportion of qualified men as qualified women. It should also mistakenly select the same proportion of unqualified men as unqualified women. Equality of Opportunity checks only the first of these conditions.

% Formula
\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \node[below=0.1cm of a, font=\normalsize] {$\forall\, y \in \{0, 1\},\; \forall\, a, b$};

        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.02,-0.02)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.16)$) to node[pos=1, above] {actual outcome} +(0.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The formula applies separately to $Y=1$ and $Y=0$, which gives the separation criterion. On an ROC plot, a point records a model's FPR and TPR at a chosen threshold. Groups with equalized odds have the same pair of rates, although they may need different thresholds to reach it.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use it when both missed positives and false alarms matter, as in fraud detection. If only missed positives are the concern, Equality of Opportunity is a narrower check. If the priority is equally reliable positive predictions, examine Predictive Parity too; matching error rates does not guarantee matching precision.


\coloredboxes{
\item Checks both positive and negative cases, so equal recall cannot hide a difference in false-positive rates.
\item Uses familiar confusion-matrix rates, making it possible to identify which type of error differs between groups.
}
{
\item Matching both rates can require group-specific thresholds or randomized decisions, and may reduce accuracy.
\item When base rates differ and TPR and FPR are both nonzero, matching those rates gives different precision across groups.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{figures/Equality_of_Odds.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Equality of Odds.}{Each square is one person in a simulated screening task. Qualified people occupy the upper outlined block; unqualified people are below. The model selects $50$ of $60$ qualified people in Group A and $25$ of $30$ in B: about $83\%$ each. It selects four of $40$ unqualified people in A and seven of $70$ in B: $10\%$ each.}

Both TPR and FPR match, so equality of odds holds. There are still more false positives in B: the same $10\%$ rate applies to $70$ unqualified people there, compared with $40$ in A.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Hardt, Price, and Srebro's $2016$ method can achieve equalized odds after training. It uses each group's scores and labels to find decision rules that match both error rates while minimizing prediction loss. Rules may use different thresholds by group or randomly choose between thresholds. Matching rates may cost accuracy. The underlying scores remain unchanged; only their use changes.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Equality of Odds includes Equality of Opportunity because it requires equal TPR as well as equal FPR. Predictive Parity checks precision instead, so compare it separately. Demographic Parity checks overall selection rates, which can differ even when both error rates match.

\metricentry{Predictive Parity}{Predictive Parity}\label{sec:predictive-parity}

Predictive parity requires positive predictions to be correct equally often across groups. For a loan model, we look at the applicants it approves: if $80\%$ of approved men repay, $80\%$ of approved women should repay too. Here we start with the approved applicants, rather than everyone who would repay. The proportion who repay is the model's precision, also called positive predictive value (PPV).

% Formula
\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-5.01,-0.16)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {actual outcome} +(0.70,0.35);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.82,-0.03)$) to node[pos=1, above] {predicted outcome} +(0.00,0.25);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.56,0.18)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1.00,-0.36);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The formula considers only people who receive a positive prediction ($\hat{Y}=1$). If positive predictions are correct equally often, they are also wrong equally often: the false discovery rate is $FDR=1-PPV$. Predictive parity checks one part of sufficiency. For binary decisions, full sufficiency also requires negative predictions to be correct equally often across groups.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use it when the correctness of positive predictions matters across groups, such as approvals or alerts that lead to further action. Check Equality of Opportunity to see who the model misses. If both error rates matter, also check Equality of Odds rather than assuming equal precision covers them.


\coloredboxes{
\item Gives a direct comparison of positive predictions: a PPV of $0.8$ means $80\%$ are correct in each group.
\item Can be calculated from each group’s true-positive and false-positive counts without access to prediction scores.
}
{
\item Conflicts with Equality of Odds when base rates differ and both TPR and FPR are nonzero.
\item Equal precision can hide unequal recall: the model may overlook a much larger share of positive cases in one group.
}

Both groups have the same TPR and FPR, but B has fewer qualified people. False positives therefore make up more of its selections.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{COMPAS estimates reoffending risk in criminal justice. In $2016$, ProPublica reported higher false-positive rates for Black than white defendants. Developer Northpointe responded that high-risk defendants had similar reoffending rates across racial groups. These findings concern different criteria: error rates and predictive parity. Alexandra Chouldechova's $2017$ analysis explained their conflict when base rates differ; it did not settle whether the tool or its use was fair.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Predictive Parity compares precision, while Equality of Opportunity compares recall and Equality of Odds also checks false-positive rates. Calibration within Groups checks probability scores; those scores can be calibrated even when precision differs after thresholding.

\metricentry{Calibration within Groups}{Calibration within Groups}\label{sec:calibration-within-groups}

Calibration within groups means that predicted probabilities match observed outcome rates in each group. Suppose a loan model gives some borrowers a $30\%$ probability of repaying. Among men given that score, about $30$ out of $100$ should repay; the same should hold for women given that score. Matching the two groups is not enough: if $50\%$ repay in both, the $30\%$ prediction is wrong for both.

% Formula
\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}S} = s,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = s \quad \forall\, s,\, a
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-3.23,0.17)$) to node[pos=1, below] {actual outcome} +(0.00,-0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-1.46,-0.04)$) to node[pos=1, above] {predicted score} +(0.00,0.28);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.14,-0.04)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {protected attribute} +(1.00,0.30);
    }
\end{metricformula}

In the loan example, $S=s$ is the predicted probability of repayment, and $Y=1$ means the borrower repaid. For $s=0.3$, the formula requires an outcome rate of $30\%$ in every group. In practice, we usually group nearby scores into bins and compare their average predicted probability with the observed rate.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use it when decisions depend on estimated probabilities, such as the chance of repaying a loan. Check each group separately: overestimating that chance in one group and underestimating it in another can cancel out in the overall result. When a threshold turns scores into yes/no decisions, check the resulting error rates too.


\coloredboxes{
\item Makes probabilities comparable across groups: a score of $0.3$ should correspond to a $30\%$ outcome rate in each.
\item Checks scores across their range, rather than assessing decisions at just one chosen threshold.
}
{
\item Does not guarantee equal selection rates, precision, or error rates after a threshold is applied.
\item Requires enough outcomes within each group and score range. Small samples or broad bins can hide calibration errors.
}

\metriccaption{Figure. Calibration within Groups.}{Scores are grouped into ranges; each filled square is $10\%$ (rounded) of the people in a range who turn out to be qualified. The black line marks the range's midpoint, where a calibrated score would put the fill. The scores are calibrated for both groups combined, yet Group A (cyan) sits above the line and Group B (purple) below it.}

For scores between $0.5$ and $0.6$, about $70\%$ of Group A is qualified, compared with $38\%$ of Group B. The model therefore understates the probability for A and overstates it for B, and the same happens in every range. The scores are calibrated overall but not within these groups.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan showed in $2017$ that group calibration can conflict with two other requirements: equal average scores among actual positive cases across groups, and equal average scores among actual negative cases. All three can hold together only with equal base rates or perfect prediction. This result concerns the scores themselves. Even with calibrated probabilities, an imperfect model can therefore assign different average scores to the groups' positive or negative cases.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Calibration within Groups implies score-level sufficiency: among people with the same score, outcome rates do not depend on group. Predictive Parity instead pools everyone above a decision threshold, where groups may have different mixes of scores. Use Equality of Odds to assess the resulting error rates, and group-level calibration plots alongside the Brier Score to assess probability predictions.
