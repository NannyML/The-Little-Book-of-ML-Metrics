\chapteropener{Bias \& Fairness}{coralhard}{coralaccent}{%
A model can be accurate and still treat two groups differently. The five criteria here are conditional independence statements between
the prediction, the true label and a protected attribute, and they disagree by design. Demographic parity looks at who is selected;
equality of opportunity and equality of odds at the error rates within each true class; predictive parity and calibration at what a
positive prediction, or a score, means once it is given. When base rates differ between groups no classifier can satisfy all of them,
and the chapter's one shared example shows the same model passing two criteria and failing three. Choosing a fairness metric is
choosing which of those failures to accept.}{In this chapter}

% ---------- Demographic Parity ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{biasfairnesstyle}
\section{Demographic Parity}\label{sec:demographic-parity}
\subsection{Demographic Parity}

% reference:
% https://developers.google.com/machine-learning/crash-course/fairness/demographic-parity
% https://afraenkel.github.io/fairness-book/content/05-parity-measures.html#demographic-parity
% https://fairmlbook.org/classification.html

In a hiring screen that advances $30\%$ of applicants, demographic parity asks that the advance rate be the same for every demographic
group, regardless of who is actually qualified. It measures whether a model’s positive predictions are independent of a protected attribute
such as gender, race, or age. Because it looks only at selection rates and never at the ground truth, parity can be satisfied by a model
that is accurate for one group and inaccurate for another.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b) \quad \forall\, a, b
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-1.5,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {predicted outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.3,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

This is the independence criterion: the prediction $\hat{Y}$ carries no information about the group $A$. Employment audits often apply the
four-fifths rule, flagging a model when the lowest group’s selection rate falls below $80\%$ of the highest.

\textbf{When to use Demographic Parity?}

Use it when equal representation in the selected set is the goal --- diverse candidate shortlists, outreach campaigns, or fixed slots across
groups --- especially when the historical labels are themselves suspect. Avoid it when the positive label is legitimately earned at different
rates per group (use Equality of Opportunity), or when a positive prediction must be equally trustworthy per group (use Predictive Parity).

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Needs only predictions and group labels, not ground truth, so it can be audited on live traffic where labels arrive late or never.
\item Maps onto legal standards: the four-fifths rule used in employment law is a demographic-parity test.
}
{
\item Ignores correctness. If one group is $60\%$ qualified and another $30\%$, forcing both to the same rate means selecting unqualified members of the second to fill the quota (figure).
\item When base rates differ, enforcing parity pulls predictions away from the labels and lowers accuracy.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Demographic_Parity.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Demographic Parity.} Two groups of $100$ people, one square each, judged by the same model: it catches five of every
six qualified people and wrongly flags one in ten of the rest, in both groups. Parity looks only at the outlined block, who was flagged.
With one threshold, Group A ($60$ qualified) has $54$ flagged and Group B ($30$ qualified) has $32$, so parity fails. Lowering B's
threshold until it too flags $54$ satisfies parity, but $25$ of those $54$ are unqualified: equal rates are bought with a precision of
$54\%$ against A's $93\%$.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Because demographic parity constrains only the rate of positive predictions, a model can satisfy it without looking at the data at all:
a coin that comes up heads $40\%$ of the time, flipped for every applicant, selects each group at exactly $40\%$. The same loophole lets a
model hit its target by filling one group’s quota with its strongest candidates and another’s with its weakest. Equal selection rates are
not equal merit --- which is why parity is best paired with a metric that checks whether the selected people were the right ones.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

If a model satisfies demographic parity but fails Equality of Opportunity, it is selecting equal fractions of each group while missing the
qualified members of one --- the picks are landing on the wrong people. When parity and Predictive Parity disagree, a positive prediction
means something different for each group. Calibration within Groups checks the same concern at the level of scores rather than binary decisions.

\thispagestyle{biasfairnesstyle}
\section{Equality of Opportunity}\label{sec:equality-of-opportunity}
\subsection{Equality of Opportunity}

Among loan applicants who would in fact repay, equality of opportunity asks that the model approve them at the same rate whether they are
men or women. It restricts attention to the people who genuinely belong in the positive class and requires the true positive rate (TPR) ---
the share of those people the model correctly identifies --- to be equal across groups. A qualified applicant’s odds of being recognized
should not depend on their demographic group.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-2.0,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {predicted outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.0,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {actual outcome} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.0,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

This is one half of the separation criterion, which asks the prediction to be independent of the group within each true class. Equality of
opportunity enforces it only on the positive class ($Y=1$) and says nothing about the truly negative, so two groups can share an identical
TPR while one is flooded with false positives.

\textbf{When to use Equality of Opportunity?}

Use it when missing a qualified person is the costly error and that risk should be shared across groups --- shortlisting candidates for
interview, flagging patients for a beneficial follow-up, or admitting students who would succeed. Avoid it when false positives are also
harmful and must be balanced (use Equality of Odds), or when fairness should hold at the score level, not one threshold (use Calibration
within Groups).

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Equalizes the chance a qualified person is recognized without forcing equal approval rates, avoiding the accuracy hit parity takes when qualified fractions differ.
\item Reachable by post-processing: tune each group’s threshold until the true positive rates match, no retraining.
}
{
\item Silent on the negative class. A model can equalize TPR yet hand one group far more false positives --- equal opportunity for the qualified, unequal harm for the rest.
\item Only meaningful when one label is clearly desirable. For symmetric problems with no preferred outcome, there is no positive class to equalize.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{figures/Equality_of_Opportunity.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Equality of Opportunity.} The same two groups and model. Equality of opportunity looks only at the outlined block,
the truly qualified, and asks what share of them was caught: $50$ of $60$ in Group A and $25$ of $30$ in Group B, a TPR of $83\%$ in
both. Everything below the outline is invisible to the metric, so the four wrongly flagged people in A and the seven in B do not enter
it.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Equality of opportunity is deliberately one-sided. It constrains the true positive rate and nothing else, so a model can pass it while
flagging the truly unqualified members of one group far more often than another --- equal recall for the deserving, very different
false-positive rates for everyone else. That asymmetry is intentional: in lending or admissions, missing a qualified person is treated as
the harm worth equalizing, while the cost of a false positive falls elsewhere. The criterion, and its two-sided sibling Equalized Odds,
were introduced by Hardt, Price, and Srebro (2016).}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

If equality of opportunity holds but Equality of Odds fails, the groups are caught at equal rates when qualified but suffer unequal
false-positive rates --- the gap lives entirely in the negative class. Demographic Parity ignores the labels altogether, so a model can
satisfy parity while badly failing equal opportunity, and the reverse.

\thispagestyle{biasfairnesstyle}
\section{Equality of Odds}\label{sec:equality-of-odds}
\subsection{Equality of Odds}

% resources:
% https://arxiv.org/pdf/1610.02413
% https://mlu-explain.github.io/equality-of-odds/

A recidivism tool can clear one group and over-flag another while still looking accurate overall. Equality of odds rules this out: it
requires equal true positive rates and equal false positive rates (FPR --- the share of truly negative people wrongly flagged) across groups
at once, so a model is equally good and equally error-prone for every group.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlred}Y} = y,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \node[below=0.1cm of a, font=\normalsize] {$\forall\, y \in \{0, 1\},\; \forall\, a, b$};
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-2.0,-0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {predicted outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.0,-0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {actual outcome} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.0,0.6)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1,-.7);
    }
\end{center}

This is the full separation criterion --- within each true class, the prediction is independent of the group, and strictly stronger than
equality of opportunity (which remains if you relax it to the $Y=1$ case). Since a (FPR, TPR) pair is one point in ROC space, equalizing both
rates forces every group onto the same operating point.

\textbf{When to use Equality of Odds?}

Use it when both error types are consequential and must be balanced across groups --- criminal-risk scoring, fraud flagging, or disease
screening. Avoid it when only missed positives matter and you want an achievable constraint (use Equality of Opportunity), or when base rates
differ and predictions must be equally trustworthy per group (use Predictive Parity --- though the two cannot both hold).

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item Constrains both error types, closing the loophole equality of opportunity leaves open: no buying equal recall by dumping false positives on one group.
\item Compatible with strong accuracy when score distributions are similar --- if the groups’ ROC curves coincide, one shared threshold satisfies it at no cost.
}
{
\item Usually unreachable without degrading the model: with different score distributions, the only route to a shared operating point is to randomize or worsen the stronger group.
\item Cannot coexist with Predictive Parity when base rates differ --- equalizing error rates leaves precision unequal, and no tuning fixes both.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.76\textwidth]{figures/Equality_of_Odds.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Equality of Odds.} The same two groups and model, with both blocks outlined. Within the qualified block the model catches
$83\%$ in each group; within the unqualified block it wrongly flags $10\%$ in each ($4$ of $40$, $7$ of $70$). Both groups therefore sit
at the same point in ROC space, $(0.10, 0.83)$, and equalized odds holds; the flagged sets still differ in size and precision
(previous and next figures).

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{A classifier’s behavior on a group collapses to a single point in ROC space: its false-positive rate and its true-positive rate. Equality
of odds demands that every group sit at the same point. When the groups share an ROC curve, that is just a matter of choosing one threshold
for everyone. But when their curves differ --- as they do whenever the score separates the classes better for one group --- no single point
lies on both curves, and the only way to meet is to throw information away: randomize some predictions, or move a group to a worse point on
its own curve. Equalizing the odds can mean making a good model deliberately worse.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Relax equality of odds to the positive class alone and Equality of Opportunity remains. Predictive Parity asks whether a positive prediction
is equally trustworthy per group, and is mutually exclusive with equalized odds under different base rates; Demographic Parity ignores the
labels entirely.

\thispagestyle{biasfairnesstyle}
\section{Predictive Parity}\label{sec:predictive-parity}
\subsection{Predictive Parity}

% references:
% https://fairware.cs.umass.edu/papers/Verma.pdf
% https://njoselson.github.io/pdfs/ProPublica_Commentary_Final_070616.pdf
% https://afraenkel.github.io/fairness-book/content/05-parity-measures.html#predictive-value-parity-ppv-parity-and-npv-parity

When a credit model approves an applicant, predictive parity asks that the approval be equally trustworthy across groups: of everyone the
model flags as positive, the fraction who truly are should match for men and women alike. That fraction is the positive predictive value
(PPV), the same quantity as precision --- the share of positive predictions that turn out correct. Predictive parity holds when a ``yes'' from
the model carries the same meaning no matter the group.

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}\hat{Y}} = 1,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = b)
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-2.0,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.0,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted outcome} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.0,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

This also equalizes the false discovery rate (FDR) --- the share of positive predictions that are wrong --- since $FDR = 1 - PPV$. Predictive
parity is the binary-decision form of the sufficiency criterion: the true outcome is independent of group once you condition on what the
model predicts.

\textbf{When to use Predictive Parity?}

Use it when a positive decision triggers a real commitment that must be equally reliable per group --- extending credit, releasing a
defendant on a risk score, or routing patients to a scarce treatment. Avoid it when you care about catching the qualified rather than the
reliability of the flagged (use Equality of Opportunity), or when base rates differ and you would rather equalize error rates (use Equality
of Odds --- you cannot have both).

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item A positive prediction means the same thing for everyone: if PPV is $0.8$ in both groups, a flagged person is truly positive $80\%$ of the time either way, so downstream decisions can treat it identically.
\item Checkable from the confusion matrix alone --- the predicted-positive counts and how many were right per group, no scores or thresholds.
}
{
\item Cannot hold together with Equality of Odds when base rates differ --- a proven impossibility, not a tuning problem, and the crux of the COMPAS dispute.
\item Equal precision hides unequal recall: two groups can share a PPV while the model overlooks far more qualified people in one.
}

\textbf{Figure. Predictive Parity.} The same two groups and model, restricted to the outlined block of flagged people. Of Group A's $54$,
$50$ are truly qualified (PPV $93\%$); of Group B's $32$, only $25$ are (PPV $78\%$). The error rates are identical, yet a ``yes'' is right

true hits. Predictive parity fails for the very model that satisfies equalized odds.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The COMPAS recidivism tool set off the most famous dispute in fairness research. ProPublica (2016) showed that black defendants faced higher
false-positive rates, a violation of equalized odds; Northpointe replied that the tool had equal predictive value across races, satisfying
predictive parity. Both were right. Chouldechova, and independently Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan, soon proved that when base rates
differ between groups no classifier can satisfy predictive parity and equalized odds at once, short of perfect prediction. The disagreement
was not a flaw in COMPAS but a mathematical certainty, and it forces practitioners to choose which fairness they are willing to give up.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Predictive Parity constrains precision; Equality of Opportunity constrains recall (TPR); Equality of Odds constrains both error rates. When
base rates differ, predictive parity and equalized odds cannot both hold, so a disagreement between them is guaranteed rather than
informative --- the choice of which to enforce is yours. Calibration within Groups is the score-level form of the same sufficiency idea.

\section{Calibration within Groups}\label{sec:calibration-within-groups}
\subsection{Calibration within Groups}

A health system uses a risk model to enroll patients in a care program and acts on the scores directly: everyone above $0.3$ gets contacted.
Calibration within groups requires that a score mean the same thing for every group --- among all patients scored $0.3$, the same fraction
should truly be high-risk whether they are old or young. Formally, for any score value $s$ and group $a$,

% Formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P({\color{nmlred}Y} = 1 \mid {\color{nmlpurple}S} = s,\, {\color{nmlcyan}A} = a) = s \quad \forall\, s,\, a
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-1.5,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.5,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted score} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.5,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {protected attribute} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

This is the sufficiency criterion in score form: once you know the score, the true outcome carries no further information about the group. It
is what lets one threshold serve everyone --- if a score of $0.3$ means $30\%$ risk for one group and $45\%$ for another, the same cutoff
silently treats them differently.

\textbf{When to use Calibration within Groups?}

Use it when the score itself drives decisions --- risk-based pricing, triage thresholds, or any policy that reads probabilities rather than
yes/no labels --- especially when different teams set their own cutoffs downstream. Avoid it when you only ship hard classifications and
balancing error rates matters more (use Equality of Odds), or when the goal is equal selection regardless of risk (use Demographic Parity).

\enlargethispage{\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item A calibrated score is decision-ready at any threshold: because $0.3$ means $30\%$ for every group, teams can move the cutoff without re-checking fairness.
\item Often comes for free --- a model trained on log loss or Brier score tends to land roughly calibrated, so no special step is needed, unlike equalized odds.
}
{
\item Says nothing about error rates: a model can be calibrated within both groups yet flag the truly negative members of one far more often (calibration and equalized odds cannot both hold when base rates differ).
\item Calibration does not survive thresholding --- two groups with the same curve but different score distributions get different error rates at a fixed cutoff.
}

\textbf{Figure. Calibration within Groups.} The same score model, simulated for many people. For each band of scores, ten squares per
group show how many of ten people with that score turn out to be qualified; the black line is where a calibrated score would put the

Identical error rates in both groups imply miscalibration across them whenever base rates differ.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Calibration looks like the most innocent fairness criterion --- who could object to scores that mean what they say? --- yet it collides with
the others just as hard. Calibration is a form of the sufficiency criterion, while equalized odds is the separation criterion, and the same
impossibility result that settled the COMPAS debate shows the two cannot both hold whenever group base rates differ. A model can be flawlessly
calibrated within every group and still send one group far more false positives than another. ``The scores are fair'' and ``the errors are fair''
are, in general, different and incompatible promises --- and choosing calibration is choosing which one to keep.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Calibration within Groups is the score-level cousin of Predictive Parity --- both are forms of sufficiency. It trades off against the
separation criteria (Equality of Opportunity and Equality of Odds) when base rates differ. For calibration quality on its own, see the Brier
Score in the Classification chapter.
