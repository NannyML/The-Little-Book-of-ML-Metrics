A classifier turns a score into a decision, and every metric here is a view of the confusion matrix that decision produces: four
counts, and the rates, ratios and means built from them. Accuracy, precision, recall and their F-combinations describe one threshold;
ROC AUC and PR AUC sweep all thresholds; log loss and the Brier score judge the scores themselves, before any threshold; MCC, Cohen's
kappa and balanced accuracy correct for class imbalance and chance agreement; expected cost puts a price on each cell. Which one to

every other page is a function of its four cells.}{In this chapter}

% ---------- Confusion Matrix ----------
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Confusion Matrix}
\subsection{Confusion Matrix}
Almost every classification metric in this chapter is derived from the same four numbers: TP, TN, FP, FN. The confusion matrix lays them out as a table --- and reading it directly often reveals failure modes that a single summary score hides, like a $95\%$-accurate tumor classifier that misses half the malignant cases.

\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{center}
\tikz{
\node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
$\displaystyle
C_{\textcolor{nmlred}{i}\,\textcolor{nmlcyan}{j}}
= \sum_{n=1}^{M}
\mathbf{1}\{ \; y_n = \textcolor{nmlred}{i} \;\land\; \hat{y}_n = \textcolor{nmlcyan}{j} \;\}
$
};
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north west)+(6.2,0)$)
 to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual class $i$} +(-.5,.3);
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north east)+(-.2,0)$)
 to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted class $j$} +(.5,.3);
}
\end{center}
\vspace{-0.5em}

\textbf{When to use Confusion Matrix?}

Use the confusion matrix when you want the full per-class breakdown of correct vs incorrect predictions instead of a single summary score. It's the right tool when false positives and false negatives have different costs (medical diagnosis, fraud detection, content moderation). For model selection or hyperparameter tuning, pair it with a derived metric like F1 or MCC.

\coloredboxes{
\item Reveals asymmetric errors. A model with $90\%$ accuracy can hide $50\%$ of the minority class's failures --- the matrix shows you exactly where the mistakes live.
\item Foundation for every other classification metric. TP/FP/FN/TN are the building blocks of precision, recall, F1, MCC, specificity --- reading the matrix once tells you which derived metric to compute.
}
{
\item Doesn't summarize to a single number. For model selection, leaderboards, or hyperparameter tuning you still need a derived metric.
\item For more than 5 classes the matrix becomes a heatmap and individual cells lose signal. Switch to per-class precision/recall or a normalized confusion heatmap.
}
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

A simple 2x2 example for binary classification with the four categories: True Positive (TP), True Negative (TN), False Positive (FP), False Negative (FN).

\newcolumntype{C}[1]{>{\centering\arraybackslash}m{#1}}
\vspace{0.3em}
\begin{minipage}{0.46\linewidth}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|C{1.4cm}|C{1.4cm}|}
\hline
Actual & Predicted \\ \hline
1 & 1 \\
1 & 0 \\
0 & 1 \\
0 & 0 \\
1 & 1 \\
0 & 0 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{minipage}%
\begin{minipage}{0.54\linewidth}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{|C{1.8cm}|C{1.8cm}|C{1.8cm}|}
\hline
& Predicted 1 & Predicted 0 \\ \hline
Actual 1 & \cellcolor{nmlgreen!20}TP = 2 & \cellcolor{nmlred!20}FN = 1 \\ \hline
Actual 0 & \cellcolor{nmlred!20}FP = 1 & \cellcolor{nmlgreen!20}TN = 2 \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{minipage}

\vspace{1em}

That's the standard case. But in some real-world settings, you can't actually fill in all four cells. Predictive maintenance is the canonical example: if the model predicts a breakdown and maintenance is performed, the machine may never fail --- so you can't observe the true positive at all. The feedback is delayed indefinitely, and the resulting matrix is ``censored.''

\centering

Positive &

\hline
\multirow{2}{2cm}{\textbf{Does Machine fail in Reality?}} & 
Positive & 
Not available  &

\cline{2-4}
& 
Negative & 
Not available & 
Delayed \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}


\orangebox{%
Did you know that...}
{Normalize the matrix by rows and you get per-class recall; normalize by columns and you get per-class precision. Every metric in this chapter is just a different read of those four corners --- which is why a single matrix can replace half a dozen derived scores when you're diagnosing failures.
}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

When derived metrics disagree (accuracy is high but MCC is low), look at the matrix to see which off-diagonal cell is doing the damage. For multi-class problems beyond 5 classes, use a normalized heatmap.




% ---------- FPR ----------
\clearpage
\section{FPR}
\subsection{False Positive Rate}
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}

FPR answers: ``Of all the actual negatives, what fraction did we incorrectly flag as positive?'' In a spam filter, FPR is the rate
at which legitimate emails end up in your spam folder. In medical screening, it's the rate of healthy patients getting a false alarm.
FPR is one half of the ROC curve — the x-axis.

\begin{center}
\tikz{
\node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
{
$\displaystyle
FPR = \frac{{\color{cyan}FP}}{{\color{cyan}FP} + {{\color{nmlpurple}TN}}}
$
}
};
\draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {false positives} +(1,.5); 
% \draw[overlay,-latex,teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.1,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {mean of targets} +(1,-.5); 
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.5,-0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {true negatives} +(-1,-.5); 
}
\end{center}

FPR ranges from 0 (no false alarms) to 1 (every negative is misclassified). FPR = 1 $-$ Specificity (TNR), so a high
Specificity means a low FPR. In the ROC curve, FPR is the x-axis: a good classifier hugs the left side (low FPR).

\textbf{When to use FPR?}

Use FPR when false alarms are costly: spam filtering (legitimate email lost), fraud detection (good transactions blocked), medical
screening (healthy patients subjected to invasive follow-up). When both FPR and FNR matter, use the ROC curve to visualize the trade-off.

\coloredboxes{
\item Directly answers ``how often do we cry wolf?'' This is the metric that determines user trust in alert-based systems like fraud detection and intrusion detection.
\item Independent of prevalence. FPR depends only on how the model treats actual negatives, so it's stable even when the positive class rate changes.
}
{
\item Blind to the positive class. A model that never detects any positives (all predictions = negative) achieves FPR = 0, which looks perfect. Always pair FPR with TPR (Recall).
\item FPR alone doesn't tell you what fraction of your ``positive'' predictions are actually correct — that's Precision, a complementary metric with a very different denominator.
}

% ---------- FNR ----------
\clearpage
\section{FNR}
\subsection{False Negative Rate}
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}

FNR answers: ``Of all actual positives, what fraction did we miss?'' In cancer screening, FNR is the rate of undetected tumors.
In fraud detection, it's the rate of fraudulent transactions that slip through. FNR = 1 $-$ Recall, so minimizing FNR is equivalent
to maximizing Recall.
\begin{center}
\tikz{
\node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
{
$\displaystyle
FNR = \frac{{\color{cyan}FN}}{{\color{cyan}FN} + {{\color{nmlpurple}TP}}}
$
}
};
\draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {false negatives} +(1,.5); 
% \draw[overlay,-latex,teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.1,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {mean of targets} +(1,-.5); 
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.5,-0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,-.5); 
}
\end{center}

FNR ranges from 0 (no misses) to 1 (every positive is missed). In high-stakes domains, even a small FNR can be
catastrophic: an FNR of $5\%$ in airport security means 1 in 20 threats goes undetected.

\textbf{When to use FNR?}

Use FNR when missing a positive is the primary risk: disease screening (undiagnosed cancer), security systems (missed
threats), predictive maintenance (undetected failures). If false alarms are the bigger concern, focus on FPR instead.

\coloredboxes{
\item It directly measures the rate of missed positive cases.
\item It is critical in fields where false negatives have severe consequences.
\item Complements True Positive Rate (TPR) in assessing classifier performance.
}
{
\item It doesn't account for true negatives or false positives.
\item It can be misleading in highly imbalanced datasets.
\item It should be considered alongside other metrics for a comprehensive evaluation.
}

are Recall/Sensitivity, Specificity, Precision, F1-Score, and ROC curve.

% ---------- TPR ----------
\clearpage
\section{TPR (Recall/Sensitivity)}
\subsection{True Positive Rate (Recall/Sensitivity)}
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}

Recall answers: ``Of all actual positives, what fraction did we catch?'' Recall = $0.95$ in cancer screening means $95\%$ of tumors are
detected. Recall = 1 $-$ FNR, so maximizing Recall is equivalent to minimizing the miss rate. In any domain where missing a positive
is dangerous (disease, fraud, defects), Recall is the metric you optimize first.

\begin{center}
\tikz{
\node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
{
$\displaystyle
Recall = \frac{{\color{cyan}TP}}{{\color{cyan}TP} + {{\color{nmlpurple}FN}}}
$
}
};
\draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true positives} +(1,.5); 
% \draw[overlay,-latex,teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.1,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {mean of targets} +(1,-.5); 
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.5,-0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false negatives} +(-1,-.5); 
}
\end{center}

Recall ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect recall (all positive instances were correctly identified), and 0 indicates the worst
possible recall (no positive instances were identified).

\textbf{When to use TPR?}

Use Recall in medical screening (catch every disease), fraud detection (catch every fraudulent transaction), security
(detect every threat). Avoid as the sole metric — a model predicting everything as positive achieves Recall = $1.0$ while
being useless. Always pair with Precision or use F1 as a combined measure.

\coloredboxes{
\item The go-to metric when missing a positive is the worst outcome. In medical diagnosis, a Recall of $0.95$ means $5\%$ of cases go undetected — the clinical implication of that number is immediately clear.
\item Independent of the negative class. Recall depends only on TP and FN, so it's stable even when the number of negatives changes dramatically.
}
{
\item Easily gamed. Predicting everything as positive gives perfect Recall ($1.0$) but floods the system with false positives. The Precision-Recall trade-off is inescapable.
\item Ignores true negatives entirely. A model that correctly rejects $99\%$ of negatives gets no credit from Recall. For a complete picture, combine with Specificity or use MCC.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{TNR (Specificity)}
\subsection{True Negative Rate (Specificity)}

Specificity answers: ``Of all actual negatives, what fraction did we correctly identify as negative?'' It's the mirror of Recall —
while Recall measures how well you detect positives, Specificity measures how well you reject negatives. TNR = 1 $-$ FPR.
In a drug test, high Specificity means few false accusations; in email filtering, it means few legitimate emails marked as spam.

\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
        {
        $\displaystyle
        TNR = \frac{{\color{cyan}TN}}{{\color{cyan}TN} + {{\color{nmlpurple}FP}}}
        $
        }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true negatives} +(1,.5); 
        % \draw[overlay,-latex,teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.1,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {mean of targets} +(1,-.5); 
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.5,-0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false positives} +(-1,-.5); 
    }
\end{center}

TNR ranges from 0 to 1, where 1 indicates perfect specificity (all negative instances correctly identified) and 0 (no negative instances were correctly identified).
In hypothesis testing, specificity is inversely related to the Type I error rate.

\textbf{When to use TNR?}

When you need to assess a test's ability to identify negative cases correctly or when the cost of false positives is high (e.g., unnecessary medical procedures).

\coloredboxes{
\item It directly measures the ability to correctly identify negative cases.
\item It is crucial in scenarios where false positives have significant consequences.
\item High specificity is valuable for confirmatory tests.
}
{
\item It doesn't account for false negatives or true positives.
\item A test with high specificity but low sensitivity might miss many positive cases.
\item It should be considered alongside sensitivity for a comprehensive evaluation.
}

\clearpage

\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Accuracy}
\subsection{Accuracy}

Accuracy answers the simplest possible question: ``What fraction of predictions were correct?'' If accuracy is $0.92$, the model
gets it right 92 times out of 100. Its simplicity is both its greatest strength and its most dangerous weakness — accuracy
can be fatally misleading on imbalanced datasets (see the accuracy paradox below).

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Accuracy = \frac{{\color{nmlred}TP}+{\color{cyan}TN}}{{\color{nmlred}TP} + {{\color{cyan}TN}} + {\color{nmlpurple}FP} + {\color{teal!70!green}FN}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2, -0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true negatives} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2, 0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false positives} +(-1,-.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.7, -0.1)$) to[bend right=20] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(3.5,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {false negatives} +(1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

Accuracy ranges from 0 to 1. But here's the trap: on a dataset with $99\%$ negatives, a model that always predicts ``negative''
achieves $99\%$ accuracy while being completely useless. This is the \textbf{accuracy paradox} — high accuracy can mask total failure on the minority class.

\textbf{When to use Accuracy?}

Use accuracy only when your classes are roughly balanced AND all errors are equally costly. Good for: multi-class image classification
(ImageNet), sentiment analysis with balanced datasets. Avoid for: fraud detection ($0.1\%$ positive), medical diagnosis, churn prediction — use
F1, PR AUC, or Balanced Accuracy instead.

\coloredboxes{
\item Universally understood. ``The model is $94\%$ accurate'' needs no explanation. This makes accuracy the default metric for non-technical stakeholder communication.
\item Correct for balanced multi-class problems. When you have 10 balanced classes, accuracy directly reflects per-class performance.
}
{
\item The accuracy paradox. On a $99\%$ negative dataset, predicting all negative gives $99\%$ accuracy. Always check: ``what's the accuracy of a trivial baseline?'' If yours is barely higher, the model adds no value.
\item Treats all errors equally. Missing a cancer diagnosis (FN) counts the same as a false alarm (FP). When error costs differ, use metrics that distinguish error types (Precision, Recall, F1).
}

These metrics provide a more nuanced view of classifier performance across all classes.

% ---------- Balanced Accuracy ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Balanced Accuracy}
\subsection{Balanced Accuracy}

Balanced accuracy is the metric you reach for first whenever class balance might be inflating your accuracy score. Take a $95\%$ negative class: a model that predicts ``negative'' for everything scores $95\%$ accuracy --- but it's useless. Balanced accuracy fixes this by averaging how well the model does on each class, so the trivial all-negative model scores $50\%$.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Balanced\ Accuracy = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\color{nmlcyan}TP}}{{\color{nmlcyan}TP} + {\color{nmlpurple}FN}} + \frac{{\color{cyan}TN}}{{\color{cyan}TN} + {\color{nmlpurple}FP}}\right)
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.5,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(3.5,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true negatives} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.5,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {misclassifications} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

Balanced accuracy ranges from 0 to 1, with $0.5$ as the ``random or single-class-prediction'' baseline. On balanced data, it reduces to regular accuracy; on imbalanced data, the gap between the two reveals how much the majority class is inflating your accuracy score.

\textbf{When to use Balanced Accuracy?}

Use balanced accuracy for fraud detection, medical screening, defect detection, anomaly detection --- anywhere your class distribution is skewed by more than 70/30. Avoid balanced accuracy when false positives and false negatives have very different costs (use F-beta or MCC), or when one class matters much more than the others (use a weighted average of per-class recall).

\coloredboxes{
\item Robust to class imbalance. The 95/5 ``always predict negative'' trick scores $95\%$ accuracy but only $50\%$ balanced accuracy --- the gap immediately reveals a useless model.
\item Reduces to accuracy when classes are balanced, so it's safe to default to in any binary classification report. Cheap insurance against being fooled by skewed data.
}
{
\item Treats all classes as equally important. If missing a positive (e.g., tumor) costs 100x more than a false positive, balanced accuracy doesn't capture that --- use cost-sensitive metrics.
\item Variance bomb on tiny minority classes. With 5 positives in your test set, one extra mistake swings balanced accuracy by 10 points. Always pair with sample-size context.
}

{Balanced Accuracy is mathematically equivalent to the macro-averaged recall. In scikit-learn, you can verify this:

\textbf{Other related metrics}

When balanced accuracy and accuracy diverge by more than a few points, your data is imbalanced and accuracy is misleading. For a single metric that handles all four confusion-matrix corners (not just the per-class recall), use MCC. For threshold tuning on imbalanced data, PR AUC is more informative than ROC AUC. If class costs differ, use cost-sensitive accuracy or F-beta with tuned $\beta$.

% ---------- Precision ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Precision}
\subsection{Precision}

Precision answers: ``When my model says positive, how often is it right?'' If Precision is $0.80$, then $80\%$ of the items flagged
as positive are truly positive, and $20\%$ are false alarms. Precision is critical when the cost of acting on a false positive is high
— approving a fraudulent loan, recommending an irrelevant product, or sending a patient for unnecessary surgery.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Precision = \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}TP}}{{\color{nmlcyan}TP} + {\color{nmlpurple}FP}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.4, 0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true positives} +(1,.5); 
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2, -0.05)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false positives} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

Precision ranges from 0 to 1. A deceptive edge case: a model that predicts positive only once and gets it right has
Precision = $1.0$ — technically perfect but practically useless. This is why Precision must always be paired with Recall.

\textbf{When to use Precision?}

Use Precision when false positives are costly: spam filtering (losing legitimate email), content moderation (censoring valid content),
recommendation systems (irrelevant suggestions erode trust). Avoid as a standalone metric — a model can achieve perfect Precision by
being extremely conservative (predict positive rarely). Always pair with Recall or use F1.

\coloredboxes{
\item Directly measures the ``signal-to-noise ratio'' of your positive predictions. Precision = $0.80$ means $80\%$ signal, $20\%$ noise.
\item Independent of the number of actual negatives. Precision stays meaningful even on extremely imbalanced datasets where accuracy would be misleading.
}
{
\item Blind to false negatives. A model that detects only 1 out of 1,000 fraudulent transactions but gets that one right has Precision = $1.0$. Always pair with Recall.
\item It can be misleading when used in isolation, as a model that predicts only one sample as positive and correctly gets it would have perfect precision.
}

    \centering

\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Precision.} Precision as a function of TP and FP. Precision is highest when FP is low relative to TP.

\orangebox{%
Did you know that...}
{
    Precision and Recall often have an inverse relationship. As you increase Precision, Recall tends to decrease, and vice versa.
    This is known as the Precision-Recall trade-off curve.
}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Precision is most commonly used alongside Recall. To combine both into a single metric, use the F1-score or the F-beta score.
For a threshold-independent evaluation, consider the PR AUC.

% ---------- F1-score ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{F1-score}
\subsection{F1-score}

F1 combines Precision and Recall into a single number using the harmonic mean. Why harmonic, not arithmetic? Because it
punishes imbalance: a model with $100\%$ Precision and $1\%$ Recall gets an arithmetic mean of $50.5\%$ (misleadingly high) but an
F1 of just $0.02$ (correctly terrible). F1 forces both metrics to be high — you can’t game it by sacrificing one for the other.


% formula
\vspace{0.5em}
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                F1 = \frac{2}{\textcolor{nmlcyan}{P^{-1}} + \textcolor{nmlpurple}{R^{-1}}} = \frac{2\textcolor{nmlred}{TP}}{2\textcolor{nmlred}{TP} + \textcolor{nmlgreen}{FP} + \textcolor{gray}{FN}}
                $
            }
        };
    \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-2.7, 0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {\color{nmlcyan} precision} +(-1, -.5);
    \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.2, 0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {\color{nmlpurple} recall} +(-0.5, -.5);
    \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlgreen, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.3, 0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {\color{nmlgreen} false positive} +(0.5, -.5);
    \draw[overlay,-latex, gray, semithick] ($(a.south)+(3.4, 0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {\color{gray} false negative} +(1, -.5);
    \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.3, -0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {\color{nmlred} true positive} +(1, .5);
    }
\end{center}
\vspace{0.5em}

The F1-score ranges from 0 to 1. Maximizing precision and recall allows us to approximate a perfect classifier. However, bear in mind that in practice, there is a
trade-off between precision and recall, so improving precision may inadvertently decrease recall and vice versa.

\textbf{When to use F1-score?}

Use the F1-score when you need a balanced measure of a model's performance, with an equal balance between precision and recall (when false positives and negatives are concerns).

\coloredboxes{
\item F1-score provides a single, easy-to-interpret metric that equally weights precision and recall.
\item It works better than accuracy for imbalanced datasets.
}
{
\item The F1-score doesn't consider true negatives, which might be necessary in some scenarios.
\item It assumes that precision and recall are equally important. F-beta score can address this by allowing adjustable weighting between precision and recall.
}

When precision and recall are not equally important, use the F-beta score. For a metric that also considers true negatives, consider MCC or the P4-metric.

% ---------- F-beta ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{F-beta}
\subsection{F-beta}

F-beta generalizes F1 by letting you explicitly weight Precision vs Recall. Set $\beta = 2$ when missing a positive is twice as bad as
a false alarm (medical screening: catch every disease). Set $\beta = 0.5$ when false alarms are twice as bad as misses (content moderation:
don't censor valid content). F1 is the special case where $\beta = 1$ (equal weight).

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
        {
            $\displaystyle
            F_{\beta} = \frac{(1 + \beta^2) {\color{nmlred}TP}}{(1 + \beta^2) {\color{nmlred}TP} + {\color{nmlpurple}FP} + \beta^2 {\color{teal!70!green}FN}} = \frac{(1 + \beta^2) \cdot {\color{nmlcyan}P} \cdot {\color{nmlyellow}R}}{\beta^2 \cdot {\color{nmlcyan}P} + {\color{nmlyellow}R}}
            $
        }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-0.5, -0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(4.6,-0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {precision} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.8, 0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false positives} +(-1,-.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, teal!70!green, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.2,0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false negatives} +(-0.5,-.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlyellow, semithick] ($(a.south)+(4.8,0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {recall} +(1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

Similar to the F1-score, the highest possible value is $1.0$, indicating perfect precision and recall, while the lowest possible value is 0, which occurs when both precision and recall are zero.
The two most common $\beta$ values are 2, which weights recall higher than precision, and $0.5$, which, conversely, weights precision higher than recall.

\textbf{When to use F-beta?}

The F-beta Score is particularly useful when Precision and Recall are unequally important.

\coloredboxes{
\item F-beta is customizable. It allows to prioritize precision or recall based on the context of your problem.
\item Helps balancing the trade-off between precision and recall.
}
{
\item Choosing an appropriate value for $\beta$ can be non-trivial and requires domain knowledge.
\item Just like F1-score, F-beta doesn't consider true negatives, which might be necessary in some scenarios.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/F_beta_curves.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. F-beta.} F-beta vs Recall at fixed Precision=$0.8$. With $\beta=0.5$ (favors precision), the curve is highest. With $\beta=2$ (favors recall), the score increases more steeply with recall.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Common choices for $\beta$ are: $\beta = 2$ (F2-score), which weights recall twice as much as precision, useful in medical screening where missing a disease
is worse than a false alarm; and $\beta = 0.5$ (F0.5-score), which weights precision twice as much, useful in search engines where irrelevant results are costly.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

The F-beta score generalizes the F1-score. If you need a metric that also accounts for true negatives, consider MCC or the P4-metric.

% ---------- ROC AUC ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{ROC AUC}
\subsection{Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve}

ROC AUC is the most popular threshold-independent classification metric, and it has a probabilistic reading: pick one random positive
and one random negative, and AUC is the probability that the model ranks the positive higher. AUC = $0.90$ means it does so $90\%$ of
the time.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                \text{ROC AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} {\color{nmlpurple}TPR}\big({\color{nmlcyan}FPR}^{-1}({\color{nmlyellow}x})\big) \, d{\color{nmlyellow}x}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.6,-0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positive rate} +(-0.8,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.6,0.25)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {false positive rate} +(-0.6,-.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlyellow, semithick] ($(a.north)+(2.9,-0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {swept from $0$ to $1$} +(-0.4,.5);
    }
\end{center}

AUC = $1.0$ is perfect and $0.5$ is random ranking (a deliberately inverted model also scores $0.5$, but in practice AUC near $0.5$
means no discriminative power). Above $0.9$ is excellent; $0.7$--$0.9$ is good; below $0.7$ warrants investigation.

\textbf{When to use ROC AUC?}

Use ROC AUC to evaluate a model's ranking ability across all thresholds, before choosing a deployment threshold, and to compare models
(``Model A has AUC $0.92$ vs Model B's $0.87$''). Avoid it for severely imbalanced data where positives are rare ($<1\%$): use PR AUC,
since ROC AUC is misleadingly optimistic there.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
\item ROC AUC is threshold-independent. It summarizes classifier performance across all possible thresholds.
\item It allows for a probabilistic interpretation, providing a clear comparison between different models.
}
{
\item It can be misleading if the goal is to optimize performance at a specific threshold. Models can have the same AUC but vary in performance at key thresholds.
\item It summarizes performance over regions of the ROC space in which one would rarely operate, such as extreme right (high FPR) or extreme left side (low TPR).
\item ROC AUC doesn't inform about precision and negative predicted value.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}


\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/ROC_AUC_curves.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. ROC AUC.} ROC curves for a good model (AUC~0.95, blue) and a weak model (AUC~0.62, red). The dashed diagonal represents random guessing (AUC=0.50). The area under each curve is the AUC.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The ROC curve was developed during World War II for analyzing radar signals. Radar operators needed to distinguish between enemy aircraft and noise,
leading to the development of signal detection theory and the receiver operating characteristic.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

For imbalanced datasets where the positive class is rare, PR AUC is often preferred over ROC AUC. For a single-threshold evaluation, consider the F1-score or MCC.

% ---------- PR AUC ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{PR AUC}
\subsection{Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve}

PR AUC is the metric of choice for imbalanced datasets where ROC AUC can be misleadingly optimistic. On a dataset with 1\% positives,
a model that catches 90\% of positives but generates 10x as many false positives can still have ROC AUC = 0.95 — yet its PR AUC will be much
lower, correctly exposing the flood of false alarms. PR AUC directly measures how useful your positive predictions are.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
        {
            $\displaystyle
            PR-AUC = \int_{{\color{nmlyellow}0}}^{{\color{nmlyellow}1}} {\color{nmlcyan}P}({\color{nmlyellow}R}) \, d{\color{nmlyellow}R}
            $
        }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3, -0.3)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {precision} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlyellow, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.9,-0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {recall} +(1,.5);
    }
\end{center}

Similar to the F1-score, the highest possible value for PR AUC is 1.0, indicating perfect precision and recall, while the lowest possible value is 0.
The main difference between PR AUC and ROC AUC is how they treat true negatives. PR AUC doesn't consider true negatives, which is why it is preferred over ROC AUC
when true negatives are not relevant to the problem.

\textbf{When to use PR AUC?}

Similarly to ROC AUC, PR AUC is ideal when you need a threshold-independent measure to evaluate and compare different classifiers across various operating points. 
It is particularly useful in imbalanced datasets, where one class (typically the positive class) is much rarer than the other. In such cases, PR AUC provides a more
informative measure of a model's ability to detect the minority class without being overly influenced by the majority class.

\coloredboxes{
\item PR AUC highlights performance on the minority class, making it invaluable for imbalanced datasets.
}
{
\item PR AUC doesn't provide insights into the true negative predictions.
\item It summarizes performance over regions of the PR space in which one would rarely operate.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/PR_AUC_curves.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. PR AUC.} PR curves for a good model (PR AUC~0.85) and a weak model (PR AUC~0.30). A good model maintains high precision even at high recall.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Unlike ROC AUC where a random classifier always scores 0.5, the baseline for PR AUC depends on the class distribution. For a dataset with 10\% positive class,
a random classifier's PR AUC baseline is approximately 0.1, not 0.5. This makes PR AUC particularly sensitive to class imbalance.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

PR AUC is complementary to ROC AUC. For a single-score summary that balances precision and recall at a fixed threshold, use the F1-score.
For probability calibration assessment, consider the Brier Score or Log Loss.

% ---------- Brier Score Loss ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Brier Score Loss}
\subsection{Brier Score Loss}

The Brier Score is MSE applied to probabilities: it measures the mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual
outcomes (0 or 1). If your model says ``70\% chance of rain'' and it doesn't rain, the Brier Score for that prediction is $(0.7 - 0)^2 = 0.49$.
A well-calibrated model (where 70\% predictions actually happen 70\% of the time) achieves a low Brier Score.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
        {
            $\displaystyle
            {Brier\ Score} = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} ({\color{nmlpurple}y_i} - {\color{nmlcyan}p_i})^2
            $
        }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.7, -0.3)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.7,0.5)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted probability} +(1,-.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.2,0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of samples} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

The Brier Score ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates perfect probabilistic predictions, and 1 means worst possible predictions. Because of its similarity with the MSE,
which can be decomposed as the sum of the variance and the squared of the bias. The Brier Score Loss can be decomposed as the sum of calibration loss and refinement loss.
For multi-class classification, the Brier Score can be extended to: ${Brier\ Score} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{C} (y_{ij} - p_{ij})^2$ where $C > 2$ is the number of classes.


\textbf{When to use Brier Score Loss?}

When probability calibration is as important as the classification accuracy. Like when the problem requires reliable probability estimates for downstream tasks
like risk assessment or decision-making.

\coloredboxes{
\item Summarizes both predictive accuracy and calibration in one value.
}
{
\item Originally designed for binary tasks. Multi-class extensions exist but change the score's range and interpretation.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/Brier_Score_curves.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Brier Score.} as a function of predicted probability for both classes. The score is minimized when the predicted probability
matches the actual outcome (p=1 for actual=1, p=0 for actual=0).

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The Brier Score was introduced by Glenn W. Brier in 1950 for evaluating weather forecasts. It remains one of the most widely used proper scoring rules,
meaning that it is minimized when the predicted probabilities match the true probabilities of the events.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

The Brier Score is closely related to Log Loss; both evaluate predicted probabilities. The key difference is that Log Loss more heavily penalizes
confident wrong predictions. For classification-based (non-probabilistic) evaluation, consider accuracy or F1-score.

% ---------- Log Loss ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Log Loss}
\subsection{Log Loss}

Log Loss brutally punishes confident wrong predictions. If your model says ``99\% chance this is spam'' and it's not spam,
Log Loss for that prediction is $-\log(0.01) = 4.6$ — a massive penalty. Compare this to Brier Score which would give
$(0.99)^2 = 0.98$. This makes Log Loss the go-to loss function for training classifiers, and a powerful metric for evaluating
probability calibration.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
        {
            $\displaystyle
            {Log\ Loss} = -\frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} ({\color{nmlpurple}y_i} \cdot log({\color{nmlcyan}p_i}) + (1 - {\color{nmlpurple}y_i}) \cdot log(1 - {\color{nmlcyan}p_i}))
            $
        }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-1.3, -0.3)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual outcome} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.4,-0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {predicted probability} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-2.6,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of samples} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

As is common with loss metrics, smaller values are better. Log Loss ranges from 0 to $\infty$, where 0 indicates perfect probabilistic predictions and higher values
represent worse predictions. The formula above defines Log Loss for binary classification, but it can also be extended to multiclass classification:  
$Log Loss = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{C} y_{ij} log(p_{ij})$.
One key difference between Log Loss and Brier Score is that Log Loss is more sensitive to differences in predicted probabilities. This can be either an advantage or a
disadvantage, depending on the use case.


\textbf{When to use Log Loss?}

When you want to evaluate both the accuracy and confidence of predicted probabilities. Similar to the Brier Score, Log Loss is also useful when calibration
and well-distributed probabilities are important for downstream tasks such as ranking or risk assessment.

\coloredboxes{
    \item It is also a proper loss function, commonly used in logistic regression and neural networks.
    \item Generalizable beyond binary classification.
}
{
    \item Predictions close to 0 or 1 for the wrong class can heavily increase the loss.
    \item Not suitable if the model doesn't output well-calibrated probabilities.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/Log_Loss_curves.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Log Loss.} as a function of predicted probability for both classes. Notice how Log Loss grows unboundedly as the predicted
probability moves away from the actual outcome, making it much more punishing for confident wrong predictions than the Brier Score.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Log Loss is mathematically equivalent to the negative log-likelihood of a Bernoulli distribution. This is why maximizing log-likelihood in logistic regression
is the same as minimizing Log Loss, making it the natural loss function for probabilistic binary classifiers.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Log Loss is closely related to the Brier Score; both evaluate probability calibration. For a normalized version, consider the D-squared Log Loss Score.
When probabilities are not available, use threshold-based metrics like accuracy or F1-score instead.

% ---------- Jaccard Index ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Jaccard Index}
\subsection{Jaccard Index}

The Jaccard Index measures the overlap between two sets: the size of their intersection divided by the size of their union. In binary classification this works out to $TP / (TP + FP + FN)$ --- a stricter version of accuracy that leaves out the true-negative contribution. In computer vision it goes by another name: Intersection over Union (IoU).

\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle 
                J(A, B) = 
                \frac{\textcolor{nmlpurple}{|A \cap B|}}
                {\textcolor{nmlcyan}{|A \cup B|}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.2, -0.2)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {\color{nmlpurple} Intersection} +(-1, .5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex, nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.2,0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {\color{nmlcyan} Union} +(1, -.5);
    }
\end{center}

Jaccard ranges from 0 (no overlap) to 1 (perfect overlap). Because the denominator leaves out true negatives, Jaccard doesn't get inflated when most samples belong to the negative class --- the failure mode that makes accuracy look misleadingly high on imbalanced data.

\textbf{When to use Jaccard Index?}

Use Jaccard for segmentation, document similarity, recommender deduplication, or any task framed as ``how much do these two sets overlap?'' Avoid Jaccard when true negatives carry information you care about, or when partial credit matters more than strict overlap.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
    \item Ignores true negatives. On a 1\% positive class, accuracy reads 99\% even for a useless model --- Jaccard reads 0. This is the right behavior for segmentation where ``background'' dominates.
    \item Scale-invariant. Two pixels overlapping or two thousand pixels overlapping give the same score for the same proportion --- lets you compare across object sizes in one report.
}
{
    \item Punishes partial overlap heavily. Two sets that overlap 50\% by area give Jaccard $= 1/3$, not 1/2. If you want gentler scoring, use Dice ($J = D/(2-D)$).
    \item Not a true distance metric --- fails the triangle inequality. Doesn't matter for most ML use, but matters if you're chaining set comparisons in clustering or retrieval.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Jaccard_overlap.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Jaccard Index.} Two binary masks compared. \underline{Left:} large overlap between predicted (cyan) and ground truth (purple) gives Jaccard $\approx 0.82$. \underline{Right:} small overlap gives Jaccard $\approx 0.15$. The metric drops sharply once the two sets diverge --- 50\% overlap by area gives $J = 1/3$, not $1/2$.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Jaccard and the Sørensen-Dice coefficient are monotonically related through $\text{Jaccard} = \text{Dice}/(2 - \text{Dice})$. They always rank pairs of models the same way, so picking one or the other is purely a presentational choice.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

For evaluations that include true negatives, use accuracy or balanced accuracy. The Sørensen-Dice section in the Computer Vision chapter covers the closely-related Dice coefficient.

% ---------- D-squared Log Loss Score ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{D-squared Log Loss Score}
\subsection{D-squared Log Loss Score}

D-squared Log Loss does for log loss what R² does for MSE: normalizes it against a baseline that always predicts the mean class probability. The result is on the same scale as R² and reads the same way --- $D^2 = 0.85$ means your model reduces log loss by 85\% versus the baseline.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                D^2 = 1 - \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}L_{\text{model}}}}{{\color{nmlpurple}L_{\text{null}}}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.0,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {log loss of the model} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.0,0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {log loss of the null model} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

$D^2$ ranges from $-\infty$ to 1: $D^2 = 1$ is perfect; $D^2 = 0$ means no better than predicting the training class frequencies; $D^2 < 0$ means actively worse than that baseline. A negative $D^2$ in production is a red flag --- your model has degraded below the trivial baseline and should not be serving predictions.

\textbf{When to use D-squared Log Loss Score?}

Use D-squared Log Loss when comparing probabilistic classifiers across different datasets or class distributions, where raw log loss isn't directly comparable. Avoid it when you need a threshold-based metric instead (use F1 or accuracy).

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
    \item Normalized scale enables cross-dataset comparison. A fraud model on bank A's data and bank B's data give comparable D² scores even if class balances differ --- raw log loss can't do this.
    \item Same intuitive ``0 = baseline, 1 = perfect'' interpretation that data scientists already know from R². Easy to communicate to non-ML stakeholders.
}
{
    \item Requires the trivial baseline to be computed correctly. \texttt{sklearn.d2\_log\_loss\_score} handles this; if you re-implement, use \textit{training} class frequencies (not test) to avoid leakage.
    \item Inherits log loss's sensitivity to confident wrong predictions. One $\hat{p}=0.001$ on a positive case dominates the score --- clipping probabilities (\texttt{clip(p, 1e-15, 1-1e-15)}) is mandatory.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/D2_Log_Loss_curve.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. D-squared Log Loss Score.} $D^2$ as a function of model log loss for three class-balance scenarios. The baseline $L_{\mathrm{null}}$ is the log loss of always predicting class frequencies, which is much lower on imbalanced data. The same raw log loss (say 0.4) gives $D^2 \approx 0.4$ on balanced data but a deeply negative value on heavily imbalanced data --- which is why D² is comparable across datasets in a way raw log loss isn't.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The D-squared framework generalizes R-squared to any deviance function, not just squared error. In scikit-learn, \texttt{d2\_log\_loss\_score} uses log loss as the deviance,
but you can also compute D-squared with other losses like absolute error or Poisson deviance.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

For calibration quality specifically, the Brier Score is more diagnostic. For threshold-based decision quality, use F1 or MCC. Raw log loss is still the right choice during training (it's a proper loss function); D² is for reporting.

% ---------- P4-metric ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{P4-metric}
\subsection{P4-metric}

P4 combines all four conditional probabilities of binary classification --- precision, recall, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV) --- into a single harmonic-mean-style score. Unlike F1, which is the harmonic mean of just precision and recall, P4 also accounts for how well the model handles negatives. So a model that predicts ``positive'' for everything gets F1 = 0.95 on a 95\% positive class but P4 = 0, because specificity and NPV both collapse to zero.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                P4 = \frac{4 \cdot {\color{nmlcyan}TP} \cdot {\color{cyan}TN}}{4 \cdot {\color{nmlcyan}TP} \cdot {\color{cyan}TN} + ({\color{nmlcyan}TP}+{\color{cyan}TN})({\color{nmlpurple}FP}+{\color{nmlpurple}FN})}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.2,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,cyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true negatives} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(3.0,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {misclassifications} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

P4 ranges from 0 to 1, with 0.5 marking a ``barely useful'' model and 0.8+ indicating strong performance on both classes. Unlike F1, which can stay high while the model fails on negatives, P4 collapses to 0 if any of TP, TN, FP, or FN is zero --- making it impossible to hide a single-class failure mode behind a single number.

\textbf{When to use P4-metric?}

Use P4 for binary classification when both class predictions matter equally --- medical screening, fraud detection, A/B-test classifiers. Avoid P4 when stakeholders expect F1 (P4 is less familiar) or when you need a threshold-tunable metric for selection (use MCC instead).

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
    \item All-four-corner penalty. A model with 100 TPs, 100 TNs, 50 FPs, 0 FNs gets F1 $= 0.80$ but P4 $\approx 0.69$ --- the P4 drop reflects the false-positive cost that F1 understates.
    \item Symmetric: swap class labels and P4 stays the same. F1 doesn't have this property --- it depends on which class you call ``positive,'' which can lead to inconsistent reporting across teams.
}
{
    \item Collapses to 0 if any of TP/TN/FP/FN is zero. This is a feature (it catches single-class failures) but also a brittleness: a tiny test set with one missing corner gives uninformative P4 $= 0$.
    \item Less widely adopted than F1, MCC, or balanced accuracy. Be ready to explain that it's ``F1 that includes specificity.''
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/P4_vs_F1.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. P4-metric.} For a model with fixed positive-class performance (precision $=$ recall $= 0.8$), F1 stays constant at 0.80 regardless of how the model handles negatives. P4 moves with specificity: it falls below F1 when the model fails on negatives, climbs above when it doesn't. The gap at low specificity is what F1 hides.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{P4 is the harmonic mean of four conditional probabilities --- Precision, Recall, Specificity, and Negative Predictive Value (NPV) --- which is where the name comes from. Introduced by Tharwat (2020) specifically to fix F1's blindness to true negatives.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

P4 and MCC both fix F1's TN-blindness, but differently: MCC computes a correlation (range $-1$ to 1, 0 is random); P4 computes a harmonic mean (range 0 to 1, 0.5 is barely useful). If your team is comfortable with correlation interpretation, prefer MCC. If F1 is the cultural default, P4 is the drop-in replacement that also includes the TN side. For precision/recall trade-offs, use F-beta with tuned $\beta$.

% ---------- Cohen's Kappa ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{Cohen's Kappa}
\subsection{Cohen's Kappa}

Cohen's Kappa measures the agreement between two annotators (or between a model and a single annotator) after subtracting the agreement expected by chance. Without this correction, raw agreement is misleading on imbalanced data: two annotators that label items randomly with the majority-class rate already match 68\% of the time on an $80/20$ dataset, with no actual skill. Kappa strips that chance baseline out.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                \kappa = \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}p_o} - {\color{nmlpurple}p_e}}{{\color{nmlred}1} - {\color{nmlpurple}p_e}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.5,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {observed agreement} +(1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.8,0.2)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {chance agreement} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

Cohen's Kappa ranges from $-1$ to $1$. A score of $1$ is perfect agreement, $0$ is chance-level, and negative values mean the annotators agree less often than chance. A common interpretation scale: $\kappa < 0.2$ is slight, 0.2--0.4 is fair, 0.4--0.6 is moderate, 0.6--0.8 is substantial, and $\kappa > 0.8$ is almost perfect. For ML use, $\kappa \geq 0.6$ is the typical threshold.

\textbf{When to use Cohen's Kappa?}

Use Cohen's Kappa when measuring agreement between two annotators (typically before training), or when comparing a model's predictions to a single noisy annotator. Avoid it as a primary model-selection metric --- use MCC for that. For ordinal labels where some disagreements are worse than others, use weighted kappa.

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
\coloredboxes{
    \item Chance-corrected. Random labeling on imbalanced data can produce 80\% agreement by accident --- Kappa subtracts that out, leaving only the agreement that reflects actual skill.
    \item Works for any number of categories. Doesn't require binary classification --- Kappa is well-defined for multi-class with any $k$ categories.
}
{
    \item The ``Kappa paradox'': on heavily imbalanced data, very different observed-agreement values can give the same Kappa. Always report Kappa alongside the raw agreement percentage to spot this.
    \item Not advised as the primary classifier metric. Most ML teams use accuracy/F1/MCC for model evaluation and reserve Kappa for annotation quality control.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/Cohens_Kappa_levels.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. Cohen's Kappa.} Two annotators that label items randomly with the majority-class rate. As class imbalance grows, raw observed agreement climbs toward $1$ --- they look like they're doing well --- but Cohen's $\kappa$ correctly stays at $0$. The gap between the two lines is what raw agreement hides.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Cohen's Kappa was introduced by Jacob Cohen in 1960 in his paper \textit{A Coefficient of Agreement for Nominal Scales}. Despite its popularity, Cohen himself
acknowledged its limitations and later introduced weighted kappa (1968) to handle ordinal categories where some disagreements are worse than others.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

For more than two annotators, use Fleiss' Kappa. For ordinal labels with graded disagreement, use weighted kappa. For comparing classifier predictions to ground truth, MCC is the closest analogue and is the more common choice in ML papers. A low $\kappa$ on training data is a signal that your labels are noisy --- adding model capacity won't help if the annotators themselves disagree.

% ---------- MCC ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{MCC}
\subsection{Matthews Correlation Coefficient}

MCC is arguably the single best metric for binary classification. It's the only metric that uses all four confusion matrix values
(TP, TN, FP, FN) and produces a high score only when a model does well on \textit{both} positive and negative classes. Unlike F1,
which ignores true negatives, and unlike accuracy, which can be gamed on imbalanced data, MCC gives a balanced assessment.

% formula
\begin{center}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                MCC = \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}TP} \cdot {\color{cyan}TN} - {\color{nmlpurple}FP} \cdot {\color{nmlpurple}FN}}{\sqrt{({\color{nmlcyan}TP}+{\color{nmlpurple}FP})({\color{nmlcyan}TP}+{\color{nmlpurple}FN})({\color{cyan}TN}+{\color{nmlpurple}FP})({\color{cyan}TN}+{\color{nmlpurple}FN})}}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(-0.2,-0.1)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {true positives} +(-1,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.2,-0.1)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {true negatives} +(0.4,.5);
        \draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.5,0.3)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {misclassifications} +(-1,-.5);
    }
\end{center}

MCC ranges from $-1$ (total disagreement) through 0 (random) to $+1$ (perfect). Think of it as Pearson correlation
between the actual and predicted binary labels. MCC = 0.0 on an imbalanced dataset genuinely means ``no better than random,''
unlike accuracy which can be 95\%+ while being useless.

\textbf{When to use MCC?}

Use MCC as your primary single-number metric for any binary classification task, especially with class imbalance. It should replace accuracy
as the default in medical diagnosis, fraud detection, and anomaly detection. Avoid only when you specifically need to optimize for one class
(use F1 or Recall instead) or need threshold-independent evaluation (use AUC).

\coloredboxes{
    \item The only metric using all four confusion matrix entries. A model that cheats accuracy by always predicting the majority class gets MCC $\approx$ 0, correctly exposing it.
    \item Symmetric: swapping positive and negative labels gives the same MCC. This means it's equally fair to both classes.
}
{
    \item Harder to interpret than accuracy or F1. ``MCC = 0.65'' doesn't have the immediate intuition of ``accuracy = 92\%.'' But the interpretive effort is worth the reliability.
}

\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{figures/MCC_3d_surface.png}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Figure. MCC.} as a function of TP and FP (with FN=20, TN=80 fixed). MCC rises when both TP is high and FP is low —
it cannot be cheated by optimizing only one quadrant of the confusion matrix. Notice MCC goes negative when the classifier
is worse than random.

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{A 2020 study by Chicco and Jurman (``The advantages of MCC over F1 score and accuracy'') showed that MCC is the only binary classification
metric that produces a correct assessment even when the dataset is extremely imbalanced. They recommended it should replace accuracy and
F1 as the standard metric. The ML community has been slow to adopt this, largely due to F1's familiarity.}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

If MCC is high but F1 is low (or vice versa), it signals that your model handles one class much better than the other. MCC $\approx$ F1
means performance is balanced across classes. For threshold-independent evaluation, ROC AUC is complementary.
Cohen's Kappa is mathematically related but designed for inter-annotator agreement, not classification evaluation.

% ---------- EC ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{classificationstyle}
\section{EC}
\subsection{Expected Cost}

Expected Cost bridges classification metrics and business economics. Instead of treating all errors equally (accuracy) or weighting
by class (F1), EC lets you assign explicit dollar costs to each type of mistake. Approving a fraudulent loan costs \$80K;
rejecting a good applicant costs \$40K. EC computes the expected monetary cost of the model's decisions. 

\begin{center}
\tikz{
\node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
{
$\displaystyle
EC = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}N}} \sum_{t=1}^{N} {\color{nmlpurple}C}({\color{nmlcyan}h_t}, {\color{nmlyellow}d_t})
$
}
};
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.7,0.2)$) to[bend left=30] node[pos=1, left] {number of samples} +(-0.8,-0.5);
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.3,-0.5)$) to[bend left=30] node[pos=1, right] {true class} +(1.5,0.7);
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.7,-0.5)$) to[bend right=60] node[pos=1, left] {cost matrix} +(-1.5,0.7);
\draw[overlay,-latex,nmlyellow, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.0,0.5)$) to[bend right=30] node[pos=1, right] {decision} +(1.5,-0.7); 
}
\end{center}

For each possible true class $h$ and decision $d$, a cost $C(h, d)$ is assigned. The expected cost (EC) is then computed by averaging the costs over the test samples. EC does not assume that the set of possible decisions is the set of classes. Instead, the decisions can be the actions that may be taken by the user of the system.


\textbf{When to use EC?}

EC allows assigning specific costs to each combination of true class and predicted decision, making it ideal for applications like finance and healthcare where misclassifications have different consequences. It is highly customizable and effectively handles class imbalance.

\coloredboxes{
\item It can adjust for deployment scenarios by modifying class priors and handling class imbalance.
\item It is flexible and explicitly allows to encode the relative costs of different errors in a transparent manner.
%\item Does not limit the possible decisions to only the class labels.
}
{
\item Its effectiveness depends on well-calibrated model probabilities 
\item Defining an appropriate cost matrix can be challenging and requires knowledge of the application 
%\item EC is less widely adopted, making it harder to compare results with existing benchmarks.
}


\clearpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{figure*}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{figures/EC_cost_matrix.png}
\end{figure*}

Consider a loan approval scenario with two true classes: $H_1$ (Creditworthy) and $H_2$ (Not Creditworthy), and two decisions: $D_1$ (Approve Loan) and $D_2$ (Reject Loan).

Cost Matrix: Here we have defined a cost matrix C, where we want to heavily penalize approving loans for non-creditworthy customers (false positives).

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
 & $D_1$ (Approve Loan) & $D_2$ (Reject Loan) \\
\hline
$H_1$ (Creditworthy) & $c_{11} =  0$ & $c_{12} = 40$ \\
\hline
$H_2$ (Not Creditworthy) & $c_{21} = 80$ & $c_{22} =  0$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Test Set Example: For each row we calculate the cost $C(h_t, d_t)$ incurred on sample t with true class $h_t$ and system’s decision $d_t$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Sample (t)& True Class (h) & Predicted Decision (d) & Cost Incurred \\
\hline
1 & Creditworthy & Approve Loan & $C(h_1, d_1) = 0$ \\
\hline
2 & Not Creditworthy & Approve Loan & $C(h_2, d_2) = 80$ \\
\hline
3 & Creditworthy & Reject Loan & $C(h_3, d_3) = 40$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Taking the mean over these test samples gives us the Expected Cost (EC):
\[
EC = \frac{0 + 80 + 40}{3} = 40
\]

\orangebox{%
Did you know that...}
{
    The EC metric is also known as Expected Prediction Error or Expected Loss. In speech processing, it’s called Detection Cost Function (DCF), used for tasks like speaker verification and evaluations by NIST. It's also equivalent to Negative Expected Utility in decision theory.
}

\textbf{Other related metrics}

Metrics like F1-score, AUC, and Precision-Recall are ad hoc, treating all errors equally and lacking control over error trade-offs. However, they excel in interpretation and handling class imbalances. In contrast, Expected Cost (EC) allows explicit control over the cost of different misclassifications.
