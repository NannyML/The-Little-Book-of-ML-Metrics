\chapteropener{Ranking}{corallight}{coralaccent}{%
Ranking models put items in an order, aiming to show the most useful ones first. A search engine ranks web pages according to how well they answer a query; a recommendation system ranks items according to how well they suit a user. In both cases, the position matters because people usually see the first few results before anything further down the list.

To evaluate that order, we need to know which items are useful for the query or user. We call this their \textit{relevance}, and we can record it as a yes-or-no label or a grade. The metrics in this chapter use those judgments to assess different parts of a ranking: how early the first relevant result appears, how many relevant items make it into the first few positions, or how well the list orders items with different grades.

For recommendations, we also look beyond relevance. A movie list might suit a viewer's interests but offer little variety or nothing they have not seen before. The final pages introduce ways to measure variety, discovery and how much of the available catalog a system recommends across its users.}{In this chapter}

% ---------- @K Metrics ----------
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{@K Metrics}{@K Metrics}\label{sec:k-metrics}

An @K metric evaluates the first $K$ items in a ranking. Precision@$K$ is the fraction of those items that are relevant; Recall@$K$ is the fraction of all relevant items retrieved. With three relevant results in the top five and ten relevant items overall, $Precision@5=3/5$ and $Recall@5=3/10$. Average per-query or per-user scores.

\begin{center}
    $\displaystyle Precision@K = \frac{|\text{relevant items in top-}K|}{K}$
\end{center}

Choose $K$ for the stage evaluated. A retriever might supply $100$ candidates to a reranker showing five results: Recall@$100$ checks the candidate pool, Precision@$5$ the final slots. Compare cutoffs to choose a retrieval budget.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use @K for a limited recommendation window or candidate budget. Compare systems on the same queries, eligible items and cutoff. To isolate a reranker's contribution, also hold its candidate lists fixed. Scores calculated with sampled distractors need not match scores over the full catalog.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Matches what users actually see. $Precision@5$ on a mobile carousel directly measures the relevance of the slice the user is shown.
    \item Composable across metrics. Precision, recall, MRR, MAP and nDCG can all focus on a chosen cutoff; state the calculation convention for each.
}
{
    \item Sensitive to the choice of $K$. Precision@$5$ and Precision@$20$ can order models differently; report at several $K$ or fix $K$ from the product spec.
    \item Hard cutoff at rank $K$. An item at rank $K+1$ is treated identically to one at rank $1000$, even though it nearly made the list.
    \item Precision@$K$ has a ceiling when relevant items are scarce. With three relevant items, $Precision@10$ maxes at $0.3$ even for a perfect ranker.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

    \centering

\end{metricfigure}

Precision can rise when the next item is relevant or fall when it is not, although its overall trend here is downward. At $K = 20$, precision and recall match because both divide the number of retrieved relevant items by $20$. That equality does not identify the best cutoff.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The choice of $K$ connects evaluation to the product. If an app shows five cards, Precision@$5$ measures relevance in those five slots. A larger cutoff can still be useful earlier in the pipeline, where a retrieval system needs to collect enough relevant candidates for the final selection.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Common @K variants include Precision@$K$, Recall@$K$, MAP@$K$, and nDCG@$K$. For a binary check of whether any relevant item appears in the top-$K$, see Hit Rate.

% ---------- Mean Reciprocal Rank ----------
\metricend
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{MRR}{Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR)}\label{sec:mrr}

MRR measures how early the first relevant result appears. For each query, take one divided by that result's rank: rank $1$ scores $1$, rank $5$ scores $0.2$. Average across all queries, including zero for each query with no relevant result.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                MRR = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}|Q|}} \sum_{i=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{{\color{nmlcyan}rank_i}}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.11,0.23)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of queries} +(-1.00,-0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.80,0.28)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, right] {first relevant rank} +(1.00,-0.30);
    }
\end{metricformula}

MRR ranges from $0$ to $1$. A score of $1$ means every query has a relevant result first. An MRR of $0.5$ does not imply an average first-hit rank of two: the reciprocal ranks for hits at positions $1$, $4$ and $4$ also average to $0.5$. For MRR@$K$, a query with no hit in the first $K$ results contributes zero.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use MRR when finding a first useful answer is the goal, as in known-item search or question answering. There may be several acceptable answers, but MRR only rewards the first. When users benefit from finding several relevant items, use a measure such as MAP or nDCG as well.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Mirrors known-item search. Users want the answer at rank $1$, and MRR penalizes anything below it.
    \item Bounded in $[0, 1]$ and averageable across queries with no normalization.
}
{
    \item Ignores items below the first hit. $[\textit{relevant, junk, junk}]$ and $[\textit{relevant, gold, gold}]$ score the same.
    \item Steep penalty for top-of-list misses. Rank $1$ to rank $2$ halves the score; rank $1$ to rank $10$ drops it by $90\%$.
    \item Failed queries must stay in the average. Dropping them inflates MRR; shortening the same lists cannot improve it.
}

\metricsecondpage

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{figures/MRR_ranked_lists.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. MRR.}{Six example queries, one per row. The filled cyan dot marks each query's first relevant item — the only one MRR scores. Open circles are additional relevant items that the metric ignores; small gray dots are irrelevant. The per-query reciprocal rank sits on the right; MRR averages them at the bottom. Q1 (relevant at rank $1$) and Q5 (rank $10$) differ by $10\times$ in score; Q6 (no relevant item in the top-$10$) contributes zero.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The TREC-8 question-answering track in 1999 scored systems by the reciprocal rank of their first correct answer, considering up to five responses per question. A correct answer first earned $1$, a correct answer fifth earned $0.2$, and no correct answer earned zero. The scoring rule made the goal concrete: return a correct answer as early as possible.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

For multi-relevant evaluation use MAP. For graded relevance (some items more relevant than others, e.g., a five-star rating system) use nDCG — it credits every relevant item with a position-discounted contribution, whereas MRR collapses the entire list to a single number from the first hit. For a binary top-$K$ check use Hit Rate.

\metricend
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{MAP}{Mean Average Precision (MAP)}\label{sec:map}

MAP measures how well a ranking brings relevant items toward the top. For each query, sum precision at each relevant result and divide by the total number of relevant items to get Average Precision (AP). MAP averages AP across queries. A fourth relevant item at rank $10$ contributes precision $4/10$.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                MAP = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}|Q|}} \sum_{q=1}^{|Q|} \frac{1}{|{\color{nmlcyan}R_q}|} \sum_{k=1}^{n} {\color{nmlpurple}P(k)} \cdot {\color{nmlcyan}rel(k)}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.74,0.30)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of queries} +(-1.00,-0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(3.46,-0.66)$) to node[pos=1, above] {relevant items} +(0.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.87,-0.66)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {precision at $k$} +(-1.00,0.94);
    }
\end{metricformula}

$R_q$ contains all known relevant items for query $q$; $n$ is list length, and $rel(k)$ is $1$ for relevant items, $0$ otherwise. Unretrieved relevant items contribute zero. AP is $1$ when all relevant items precede irrelevant ones. Compare with a baseline, such as shuffled rankings, on the same queries.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use MAP when several relevant results matter and relevance is binary. MAP@$K$ sums the first $K$ positions; TREC's map\_cut divides by all known relevant items, penalizing those beyond the cutoff. State your convention. Use nDCG for graded relevance.


\coloredboxes{
    \item Rewards moving relevant items ahead of irrelevant ones.
    \item Normalizes AP by the number of relevant items before averaging across queries.
}
{
    \item Binary relevance cannot express grades, unlike nDCG.
    \item Incomplete judgments can shift AP either way. State how unjudged items are treated; keep judgments fixed across models.
    \item Less directly interpretable than a proportion such as Precision@$10$.
}

\metricsecondpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/MAP_precision_strips.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. MAP.}{Two rankings with the same four relevant items. The number under each filled dot is the running precision at that rank: $1/1 = 1.00$ for the first relevant item at rank $1$, $4/6 = 0.67$ for the fourth relevant item landing at rank $6$. AP is exactly the mean of those numbers; a single position shift reshuffles the whole sum. Same recall, very different AP.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The Text REtrieval Conference (TREC) began in 1992 and helped establish MAP as a widely used retrieval measure. Its evaluations use relevance judgments for a shared set of queries, allowing systems to be compared on the same task. Those judgments are often incomplete, which is why the evaluation procedure matters alongside the score.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

For graded relevance use nDCG. For the first relevant result use MRR; for the number of relevant results in the first $K$ slots use Precision@$K$. AP scores one query, while MAP averages AP across queries.

\metricentry{Hit Rate}{Hit Rate}\label{sec:hit-rate}

Hit Rate measures the fraction of users whose top-$K$ list contains at least one labeled-relevant item. Each user contributes $1$ for a hit and $0$ for a miss, and we average those values. It ignores how many relevant items appeared and where they were within the first $K$ positions.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Hit\ Rate = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}|U|}} \sum_{u=1}^{|U|} {\color{nmlgreen}\mathds{1}}\left(|{\color{nmlcyan}R_u} \cap {\color{nmlpurple}Top\text{-}K_u}| > 0\right)
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-1.59,0.22)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of users} +(-1.00,-0.39);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.69,0.61)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {relevant items} +(-1.00,-1.20);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.94,0.61)$) to node[pos=1, below] {top-K items} +(0.00,-0.31);
    }
\end{metricformula}

A Hit Rate of $0.72$ means $72\%$ of evaluated users have at least one labeled-relevant item in their top-$K$ list. The other $28\%$ have no labeled hit there. That tells us how often the list misses the known interests in the evaluation; it does not directly measure whether users were satisfied.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use Hit Rate to check how often a top-$K$ list contains any known relevant item. It is useful when a completely unsuccessful list matters, but pair it with a metric that rewards additional hits or their positions when those also matter.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Easy to communicate. ``$72\%$ of evaluated users have a labeled hit'' gives a clear account of this part of list quality.
    \item Can be calculated with one known relevant item per user, as in leave-one-out evaluation.
    \item Clear endpoints. $0$ means no evaluated list has a labeled hit; $1$ means all do.
}
{
    \item Position-blind. A relevant item at rank $1$ scores the same as one at rank $K$.
    \item Count-blind. A list with one relevant item scores the same as a list with five.
    \item Missing labels can turn useful recommendations into apparent misses. Choose $K$ for the intended window or retrieval budget, not just to separate models.
}

\metricsecondpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{figures/Hit_Rate_vs_K.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Hit Rate.}{These illustrative values show $15\%$ of users with a labeled hit at $K = 1$ and $96\%$ at $K = 50$. Hit Rate cannot decrease when we extend the same ranked lists, because earlier hits remain included. The remaining $4\%$ have no hit in the first $50$ positions; their first relevant item could still appear later.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Recommender systems papers often call Hit Rate ``Recall@$K$'' and use the two interchangeably. They are the same metric only when each user has exactly one relevant item — the typical leave-one-out evaluation setup. With multiple relevant items per user, Recall@$K$ measures the fraction retrieved, while Hit Rate stays binary. The naming clash trips up cross-paper comparisons more often than you would expect.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

For position-aware evaluation use MRR or nDCG. For counting how many relevant items the list retrieved, use Recall@$K$. Hit Rate is the binary collapse of Recall@$K$.

\metricend

\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{CG}{Cumulative Gain (CG)}\label{sec:cg}

Cumulative Gain (CG) sums the relevance scores of the items in a recommendation list. With graded relevance — $3$ for ``perfect'', $1$ for ``marginal'', $0$ for ``irrelevant'' — $CG_K$ is the total relevance packed into the top-$K$. The catch is that CG ignores rank: a relevant item at position $1$ adds the same as one at position $K$.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                CG_p = \sum_{i=1}^{{\color{nmlred}p}} {\color{nmlcyan}rel_i}
                $
            }
        };
        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.40,-0.07)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of items} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.23,-0.54)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, right] {relevance of item $i$} +(1.00,0.30);
    }
\end{metricformula}

CG is not normalized: with grades from $0$ to $3$, the top-$K$ total cannot exceed $3K$. Reordering the same selected items leaves their total unchanged. Moving an item into or out of that selection can change CG, so it measures how much relevance we retrieved without judging the order within it.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use CG when only total retrieved relevance matters and ordering is decided downstream (a re-ranker reshuffles the list, or items are shown simultaneously). Avoid CG when position matters to the user — which covers most ranking applications. Use DCG or nDCG instead.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Simplest relevance aggregation possible: add up the scores. Trivial to compute and explain.
    \item Works with graded relevance out of the box, unlike Hit Rate or binary $Precision@K$.
}
{
    \item Order-invariant. A list with the best item at rank $1$ scores the same as one with the best item at rank $K$.
    \item Not normalized. $CG = 12$ vs $CG = 6$ across queries says nothing about which ranking was better.
    \item Falls back to a raw count of relevant items when only binary labels are available.
}

\metricsecondpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/CG_order_blind.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. CG.}{The same ten items in one order and its reverse. CG adds the relevance grades and reaches $12$ either
way: it cannot tell the two lists apart. DCG discounts each grade by $1/\log_2(i+1)$ and scores them $7.28$ and $4.38$. CG is a check on
how much relevance a list retrieved, not on where it put it.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Swapping items within the top $K$ leaves CG@$K$ unchanged, but a swap across the cutoff can change it. That distinction matters in a retrieval-then-rerank pipeline: the retriever chooses the set of candidates, while the reranker decides their order. CG can evaluate the total graded relevance of that set; DCG adds a preference for finding the best items earlier.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

DCG extends CG by discounting later positions. nDCG normalizes DCG against the ideal ranking, so scores are comparable across queries with different relevance budgets. In practice, CG is the warm-up step toward those two.

\metricend

\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{DCG}{Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG)}\label{sec:dcg}

Discounted Cumulative Gain (DCG) adds up relevance scores while giving less weight to items further down the list. In this version, an item's weight is $1/\log_2(i+1)$ at rank $i$. Rank $1$ receives full weight; ranks $2$, $10$ and $100$ receive about $0.63$, $0.29$ and $0.15$. A useful result therefore contributes more when it appears earlier.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                DCG_p = \sum_{i=1}^{{\color{nmlred}p}} \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}rel_i}}{{\color{nmlpurple}\log_2(i + 1)}}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.45,-0.19)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {relevance of item $i$} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.85,0.31)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {position discount} +(-1.00,-0.61);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The cutoff $p$ tells us how many positions to include. DCG adds the contribution from every item up to that point, whereas MRR stops at the first relevant result. DCG@$5$ can therefore distinguish two five-item lists that share the same first hit but differ in the remaining results.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use DCG when you want to reward relevance grades and early positions within a chosen list length. Scores also depend on how much relevance is available for each query. To express the result as a share of that query's best possible score, use nDCG.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Rewards both relevance and ordering. Position $1$ gets full weight; position $K$ gets $1/\log_2(K+1)$, slow enough that the tail still counts.
    \item Works directly with graded relevance, unlike MAP or Hit Rate which need binary judgments.
}
{
    \item Not normalized. Comparing raw scores across queries requires knowing their available relevance grades and the cutoff.
    \item Calculation conventions matter. Match the gain function, discount and cutoff before comparing scores from different implementations.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{figures/CG_vs_DCG.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. DCG.}{The same list scored by CG (red) and DCG (blue). Both agree at rank $1$; later contributions receive less weight in DCG. At $K = 10$, the scores are $12.0$ and $7.16$, a reduction of about $40\%$ in the position-weighted score. The reduction is the vertical gap between the curves at that cutoff, not the area of the shading.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Kalervo J\"arvelin and Jaana Kek\"al\"ainen introduced logarithmic discounting in their 2000 SIGIR paper and developed the work further in 2002. This chapter uses each relevance grade directly as its gain. Another common choice, $2^{rel}-1$, gives high grades more influence: grades $1$ and $3$ become gains of $1$ and $7$.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

nDCG divides DCG by the best possible score for each query. MAP uses binary relevance and precision at each hit, while MRR considers only the first hit.

% ---------- Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain ----------
\metricend
\clearpage
\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{nDCG}{Normalized Discounted Cumulative\\ Gain (nDCG)}\label{sec:ndcg}

nDCG divides a ranking's DCG by its ideal DCG (IDCG), calculated by placing the highest-graded eligible items first. The ideal includes relevant items the model missed, so ordering a poor selection well cannot earn full credit.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                nDCG_p = \frac{{\color{nmlcyan}DCG_p}}{{\color{nmlpurple}IDCG_p}}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.11,-0.01)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {actual DCG} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(1.10,0.06)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {ideal DCG} +(-1.00,-0.30);
    }
\end{metricformula}

With nonnegative grades and a positive IDCG, nDCG ranges from $0$ to $1$. Zero means no item within the cutoff has positive gain; $1$ means the best possible score. With linear gain, returning a grade-$1$ item when a grade-$3$ item was available gives $nDCG@1 = 1/3$. If IDCG is zero, report whether such queries are excluded or assigned a conventional score.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use nDCG@$K$ when both relevance and position matter within the first $K$ results. It supports binary labels as well as grades and expresses each query's score relative to its ideal. MRR instead asks about the first hit, while Precision@$K$ counts hits without rewarding their order.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Normalizes each query against its ideal, allowing scores to be averaged under a shared evaluation setup.
    \item Handles graded relevance natively. A five-star rating gives a richer signal than ``relevant/not'', and nDCG uses it directly.
}
{
    \item Requires labeled relevance scores. With missing or noisy labels, IDCG is wrong and nDCG inherits the error.
    \item Sensitive to the truncation $K$ in $nDCG_K$. Truncating at $K = 5$ vs $K = 100$ gives very different scores; report at the $K$ that matches the product.
}

\metricsecondpage
\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{figures/nDCG_degradation.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. nDCG.}{This simulated sequence starts with a perfectly ordered list and swaps pairs of positions. Swaps between equally relevant items leave the score unchanged. At swap $8$, exchanging a grade-$5$ item at rank $1$ with a grade-$1$ item at rank $7$ causes the large fall; swap $16$ returns grade $5$ to the top. Random swaps can improve an already disturbed ranking. The size of each change depends on the grades and positions involved.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{The TREC 2020 Deep Learning Track used nDCG@$10$ as its main measure for document and passage ranking. It captured graded relevance near the top of the results, while other reported measures covered complementary questions, including retrieval across a longer list. A chosen cutoff is part of what a benchmark asks a system to do well.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

nDCG normalizes DCG against the ideal ranking. MAP is another option for binary relevance, with different weights on early hits. Use MRR when only the first useful result matters, or Precision@$K$ when order within the selected list does not matter.

\thispagestyle{rankingstyle}
\metricentry{FCP}{Fraction of Concordant Pairs (FCP)}\label{sec:fcp}

Fraction of Concordant Pairs (FCP) measures how often a model puts two items in the user's preferred order. We count pairs ordered correctly and divide by the number of pairs we can compare. A perfect ordering scores $1$; random ordering averages $0.5$ when there are no ties.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                \mathrm{FCP} = \frac{\color{nmlcyan}N_{\mathrm{correct}}}{\color{nmlpurple}N_{\mathrm{pairs}}}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.31,-0.04)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {correctly ordered pairs} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.31,0.07)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {total comparable pairs} +(-1.00,-0.30);
    }
\end{metricformula}

$N_{\mathrm{correct}}$ counts correctly ordered pairs and $N_{\mathrm{pairs}}$ counts all comparable pairs. Here we compare pairs with a known preference and different predicted scores, excluding ties in either the ratings or predictions. Pool the correctly ordered and comparable pairs across users before dividing. The score is undefined if none remain. State the tie rule, since implementations can treat predicted ties differently.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use FCP when recovering preference order matters more than matching rating values. It works with ordinal ratings, including five-star ratings, but ignores the size of the gaps between them. With clicks, define which items the user could have seen before interpreting an unclicked item as less preferred.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Measures whether the model preserves relative preferences, without requiring accurate rating values.
    \item Ignores shifts in rating levels. Ratings $[1,2,3]$ and $[3,4,5]$ express the same ordering of three items.
}
{
    \item Checking every pair is costly: $1{,}000$ items have $499{,}500$ pairs. Sorting-based methods can count agreements faster when comparing two rankings.
    \item Treats all pairs equally. A swap between rank $1$ and rank $2$ counts the same as a swap between rank $500$ and rank $501$.
}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering

\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. FCP.}{Two rankers order the same five items, giving ten pairs to compare. \orangebox{Did you know that...}
{For two rankings without ties, FCP and Kendall's tau express the same pairwise agreement on different scales: $\tau = 2 \cdot FCP - 1$. FCP ranges from $0$ to $1$, while tau ranges from $-1$ to $1$. The identity does not generally hold for tie-adjusted versions such as Kendall's tau-b.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Kendall's tau also compares pairwise order, with variants that handle ties. nDCG weights positions and relevance grades, while MAP uses binary relevance. MRR focuses on the first relevant result.

\metricentry{Diversity}{Diversity}\label{sec:diversity}

Diversity scores a recommendation list by how dissimilar its items are from each other on average. Sum the pairwise dissimilarities $d(i, j)$ across all unordered pairs and divide by the number of pairs. With genre-based dissimilarity, a list of ten action movies scores low; a list mixing action, comedy and documentary scores higher.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Diversity = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}|L|(|L|-1)}} \sum_{i \in L} \sum_{j \in L, j \neq i} {\color{nmlcyan}d(i, j)}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.90,0.31)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {list size} +(-1.00,-0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(3.51,-0.34)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {pairwise dissimilarity} +(-1.00,0.62);
    }
\end{metricformula}

The list is $L$, and $d(i,j)$ specifies how different two items are. For a simple genre comparison, set it to $0$ for two films in the same genre and $1$ for films in different genres. Diversity is then the fraction of pairs with different genres. The double sum counts each pair twice, once in each order, so the denominator does too. With richer item descriptions, we can instead compare their feature vectors.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use Diversity alongside relevance when offering useful variety matters, such as a movie list that should cover several interests. A list can be both relevant and varied, but improving one score does not guarantee the other improves. Measure both for the systems you are comparing.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Catches a real failure that accuracy metrics miss: the ``ten of the same'' problem in collaborative filtering.
    \item Complements relevance metrics. Compare Diversity with nDCG or MAP to see which systems offer useful variety.
}
{
    \item Entirely defined by $d(i, j)$. Switching from category-based to embedding-based dissimilarity can flip the ranking of models.
    \item Does not measure usefulness. A varied list can still be irrelevant, so assess relevance separately and examine any trade-off.
}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Diversity_comparison.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Diversity.}{Two six-item recommendation lists rendered as horizontal strips, each tile colored by genre. \textit{Top:} one item per genre — Intra-List Diversity hits its maximum ($ILD = 1.00$) because every pair of items disagrees on category. \orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Reordering a list leaves this diversity score unchanged. With three action films and one each from three other genres, $12$ of the $15$ pairs differ, giving diversity $0.8$. If the action films appear first, diversity within the first three is $0$. When users see only part of a list, calculate diversity for that part too.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Diversity measures how different the items in one list are. The popularity-based Novelty score estimates unfamiliarity from how rarely items appear in interaction history. Serendipity combines relevance with unexpectedness, while Coverage measures catalog reach across many lists.

\metricentry{Novelty}{Novelty}\label{sec:novelty}

Novelty measures how unfamiliar recommendations may be to a user. The version here estimates that from item popularity: an item that appears rarely in past interactions gets a higher score. This is a proxy, not evidence that a particular person has never seen it. A rare film may already be familiar to its biggest fan.

\begin{metricformula}
\tikz{\node[inner sep=2pt,font=\Large] {$\displaystyle\operatorname{Novelty}(i)=-\log_2 P(i)$};}
\end{metricformula}

Let $P(i)$ be the fraction of historical interactions involving item $i$. An item with $P(i) = 0.5$ scores $1$ bit; one with $P(i) = 0.01$ scores about $6.6$ bits. Average item scores to evaluate a list. Zero-count items need an explicit policy, such as smoothing the counts, because $\log 0$ is undefined.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use Novelty when discovery matters, including exploratory search, and assess relevance alongside it. It is less useful for looking up a known item: finding the requested song matters more than whether it is obscure. Specify the formulation; alternatives such as $1-P(i)$ can rank whole lists differently after averaging.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Helps identify recommendations dominated by popular items; computing the score alone does not change them.
    \item Cheap to compute — just popularity counts, no per-user state.
}
{
    \item Penalizes popular items even when popular is correct. A frequently played pop song has high $P(i)$ and low Novelty.
    \item Does not measure variety. A list of ten rare items can score high even if they all belong to the same niche.
}

\begin{metricfigure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.82\textwidth]{figures/Novelty_curve.png}
\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Novelty.}{Popularity-based novelty falls as $P(i)$ rises. The reference points show an item with $P(i) = 0.01$ scoring about $6.6$ bits and one with $P(i) = 0.5$ scoring $1$ bit. The score continues to fall beyond $0.5$, reaching zero at $P(i) = 1$.}

\orangebox{Did you know that...}
{This novelty score uses self-information, an idea from information theory. Halving an item's popularity adds one bit to its score: probabilities of $0.5$, $0.25$ and $0.125$ give $1$, $2$ and $3$ bits. This explains why small popularity differences among rare items can produce large score differences.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

This Novelty score uses item popularity; Diversity compares items within a list. Serendipity additionally asks whether a relevant item is unexpected for the user, which need not mean it is unpopular. Coverage measures catalog reach.

\metricentry{Serendipity}{Serendipity}\label{sec:serendipity}

Serendipity measures whether recommendations are both useful and unexpected. We need a way to judge relevance and a baseline for what the user would expect. One approach averages their product across recommended items. Irrelevant surprises and useful but entirely predictable items both contribute little.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Serendipity = \frac{1}{{\color{nmlred}N}} \sum_{i=1}^{N} {\color{nmlcyan}rel(i)} \cdot {\color{nmlpurple}unexp(i)}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlred, semithick] ($(a.south)+(-0.53,0.34)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {number of items} +(-1.00,-0.55);
        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(1.25,-0.60)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {relevance} +(-1.00,0.78);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(2.55,0.69)$) to node[pos=1, below] {unexpectedness} +(0.00,-0.39);
    }
\end{metricformula}

Suppose a listener likes a recommended jazz track, so we assign relevance $1$. If a baseline built from their listening history would not recommend it, we assign unexpectedness $1$ too. Its contribution is then $1 \times 1 = 1$. A liked track included by the baseline has unexpectedness $0$ and contributes zero. Continuous scores in $[0,1]$ also work, giving a result in $[0,1]$.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use Serendipity for discovery, such as music browsing or exploratory search. Agree on what counts as unexpected before comparing models. It is less useful for known-item lookup, where the user wants a particular result rather than a pleasant surprise.

\coloredboxes{
    \item Rewards recommendations that combine relevance and unexpectedness, rather than either quality alone.
    \item Makes the intended kind of discovery explicit through the chosen relevance and unexpectedness scores.
}
{
    \item Depends on the choice of $unexp(i)$. Different baselines (popularity, content-similarity, history) give different Serendipity scores for the same model.
    \item An offline score does not establish satisfaction or prove that the user would never have found the item without the recommender.
}

\thispagestyle{customstyle}

\end{metricfigure}

\metriccaption{Figure. Serendipity.}{These hypothetical recommendations illustrate the product of relevance and unexpectedness. \orangebox{Did you know that...}
{Horace Walpole coined ``serendipity'' in a letter to Horace Mann dated 28 January 1754. He drew the word from \emph{The Three Princes of Serendip}, a tale whose heroes made discoveries through both accident and perceptive reasoning. The name captures the appeal of finding something valuable that we were not deliberately looking for.}

\metricheading{Related metrics}\metricsecondary

Serendipity combines relevance with unexpectedness relative to a chosen baseline. Novelty may use popularity as a proxy for unfamiliarity; Diversity measures variety within the list. Neither alone tells us whether a recommendation is both useful and surprising.

\metricentry{Coverage}{Coverage}\label{sec:coverage}

Catalog coverage is the fraction of available items recommended during an evaluation period. Recommending $1{,}200$ distinct items from a $10{,}000$-item catalog in a week gives coverage $0.12$. Each item counts once, regardless of how many users receive it. The score describes reach across lists, not the quality of one person's list.

\begin{metricformula}
    \tikz{
        \node[inner sep=2pt, font=\Large] (a) {
            {
                $\displaystyle
                Coverage = \frac{|{\color{nmlcyan}\text{recommended items}}|}{|{\color{nmlpurple}\text{total catalog}}|}
                $
            }
        };

        \draw[-latex,nmlcyan, semithick] ($(a.north)+(0.72,-0.04)$) to[bend right=15] node[pos=1, left] {unique recommended} +(-1.00,0.30);
        \draw[-latex,nmlpurple, semithick] ($(a.south)+(0.43,0.16)$) to[bend left=15] node[pos=1, left] {total catalog items} +(-1.00,-0.39);
    }
\end{metricformula}

Fix the evaluation period, users, list length and eligible catalog: extra recommendation opportunities can raise coverage without improving the model. Coverage ignores item frequencies. Entropy over recommendation shares complements it, measuring how evenly exposure is spread within a fixed catalog.

\metricheading{When to use}

Use Coverage when catalog reach matters, such as exposure for marketplace sellers or media creators. Evaluate it alongside relevance and the distribution of recommendations. A narrow reach may be appropriate for a small audience or a known-item search task; the score alone cannot tell us whether it is a failure.


\coloredboxes{
    \item System-level signal that per-list metrics miss. nDCG can be perfect while Coverage is $0.05$ — every user gets a personal top-$10$ from the same $500$ items.
    \item Cheap and label-free. Just count unique items recommended; no relevance judgments needed.
}
{
    \item Insensitive to concentration. $90\%$ of items can appear while $99\%$ of recommendations still go to a small group; inspect their shares too.
    \item Catalog-size dependent. $Coverage = 0.5$ on $100$ items is qualitatively different from $Coverage = 0.5$ on a million. Compare within a product, not across.
}
